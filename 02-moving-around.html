Moving around

How to put the cursor where you want it.

Before you can change text you have to get to it. This chapter lists every way Tuatara has of moving the cursor: by character, word, line, sentence and paragraph, to the start or end of the text, to a line you name, and back to a place you left. It also covers counts, which repeat a move, and the marks that let you return. For each part it gives the keys and the settings that change how the move behaves. Scrolling and recentring are in chapter 1.

Keys are written as chapter 1 explains: C-x is Control and x, M-x is Alt and x, RET is Return. The tables say "none" for a command that has no key and can only be run by name with M-x.

2.1 Two sets of keys

Tuatara has two sets of keys for moving. They do the same jobs, and you may use either one, or both.

The first set is the letter keys of NORMAL state: h, j, k, l and a few more. They need no Control or Alt, so your hands stay on the letters. They work only in NORMAL state, because in INSERT state a letter is text.

The second set uses Control and Alt, as Emacs does: C-f, M-f, C-a and so on. These work in both states. They are the keys to reach for while you are typing in INSERT state and do not want to leave it. The arrow keys, <home> and <end> work in both states as well. With *modal-editing* set to nil the second set is all there is.

A move never changes the text. If a move cannot go further, such as l (non-modal: C-f) at the very end of the text, the cursor stays where it is.

The mode line shows the line and the column of the cursor, L3 C4, and both count the way chapter 1 describes: the first line is 1 and the first column is 0.

2.2 By character and by line

h and l (non-modal: C-b, C-f) move one character left and right. j and k (non-modal: C-n, C-p) move one line down and up. At the end of a line, l goes on to the start of the next one, and h at the start of a line goes back to the end of the one above.

When you move down or up, Tuatara tries to keep the column you started from. If the next line is shorter, the cursor goes to its end. When you go on pressing j or k (non-modal: C-n, C-p), the cursor returns to the original column as soon as a line is long enough, so a short line in the middle does not pull you off your column. Any other key, even h (non-modal: C-b), forgets the column.

If you have turned on soft wrap (C-x T l), a long line shows as several rows on the screen. j and k (non-modal: C-n, C-p) then move one row, not one whole line.

In the hex view, which shows the bytes of a file, h and l (non-modal: C-b, C-f) move one byte instead of one character.

Try it, with a recipe

  1. Press C-x C-f, type ~/scones.txt and press RET. Press i and type these five lines, ending each but the last with RET: Scones, 2 cups flour, 4 tsp baking powder, 1 cup milk, Mix well and bake for twelve minutes. Press C-g to go back to NORMAL state.
  2. Press g g (non-modal: M-<) to go to the top. The mode line says L1 C0.
  3. Press j j. You are on line 3 (L3 C0). Press l l l and watch the column go to 3. Press h (non-modal: C-b) once to see it come back to 2.
  4. Press j j to go to the last line, then g l (non-modal: C-e) for its end. The column is 36, the length of that line.
  5. Press k (non-modal: C-p). The line above is shorter, so the cursor goes to its end, at column 10. Press j (non-modal: C-n). The cursor is back at column 36.
  6. Press C-u 3 k. The echo area shows C-u 3- while Tuatara waits for the key, then the cursor moves up three lines at once.

2.3 By word

w (non-modal: M-f) moves to the end of the next word. If the cursor is in the gap between two words, it first passes the gap and then the next word. b (non-modal: M-b) moves to the start of the word before the cursor. A count does it that many times. A negative count reverses the direction.

W and B (non-modal: C-M-f, C-M-b) move over a whole bracketed group or a quoted piece of text in one step. In a program, they move over one expression as the language reads it. Elsewhere they move over a bracketed group or a symbol. At a closing bracket they stop, because the expression around the cursor ends there. Chapter 13 describes moving over the parts of a program.

Words are looked for no further than 64 MB away, so one stray key in a huge file does not make Tuatara wait. If the limit is reached, Tuatara says so.

2.5 By sentence and paragraph

A sentence ends at a full stop, a question mark, an exclamation mark, an ellipsis (…) or an interrobang (‽) that is followed by a space or the end of a line. Closing quote marks and brackets after the stop belong to the sentence. A full stop after an abbreviation such as Dr. or e.g. does not end it. Tuatara knows a long list of those. You can add your own, or take one away that you would rather have end a sentence.

Some people type two spaces after the end of a sentence. If you do, set *sentence-end-double-space* to t, and a sentence then ends only where two spaces, a tab or a line break follow the stop. A file can say which it uses with a line sentence-end-double-space: t in its Local Variables block, and that wins for that file.

A paragraph ends at a blank line. M-} moves to the start of the blank line after the paragraph, or to the end of the text if there is none, and M-{ moves to the start of the blank line before the paragraph, or to the start of the text.

The sentence and paragraph moves have no letter keys in NORMAL state, but the Alt keys work there. A count does the move that many times, and a negative count goes the other way.

Try it, with a letter

  1. Press C-x C-f, type ~/letter-to-napier.txt and press RET. Press i and type Dear Napier, and RET RET. Then type Thank you for the weekend. The cake was lovely. We got home at six. and RET RET. Then type Please send the photos when you can. Dr. Taupo says hello. Press C-g.
  2. Press g g (non-modal: M-<). Press M-}. The cursor goes to the blank line after the first paragraph (L2 C0). Press j (non-modal: C-n) to go to the start of the second paragraph.
  3. Press M-e. The cursor moves to just after weekend.. Press M-e again, and it goes after lovely.. Press M-a to go back to the start of that sentence.
  4. Press M-} to go to the blank line after this paragraph. Press M-{ and watch the cursor go back up.
  5. Press g e (non-modal: M->), then M-a. The cursor goes to the start of Dr. Taupo says hello.. The full stop after Dr did not end a sentence.

2.6 To the start or the end of the text

g g (non-modal: M-<) goes to the very start of the buffer and g e (non-modal: M->) goes to its end. If the buffer is narrowed (C-x x n), they go to the start and end of the part shown. In the hex view, they go to the first and last byte.

These two moves leave the mark behind at the place you left, so that you can go back (see below). They do not do it while a selection is showing, nor when you give a count.

2.7 To a line

: asks for a line number in the minibuffer, and RET takes you to the start of that line. M-g g and M-g M-g do the same, and they work in both states. You may group the digits as the mode line does, 100,000,000. The mark is left where you were, unless a selection is showing.

In a large file whose lines are still being counted, a line in the first 64 MB is found at once. A line further in waits until the count has reached it. In the hex view the same keys ask for a byte offset instead, and a number that starts with 0x is read as hexadecimal.

If the line you ask for is outside the part of a narrowed buffer, Tuatara widens the buffer and says so.

Tuatara has no command that asks for a column. To get to a column, go to the start of the line and press l (non-modal: C-f) with a count, as in C-u 12 l, which works when the line is long enough.

2.8 Scrolling and recentring

The keys that scroll the window and put the cursor's line in the middle are listed in chapter 1: C-v and M-v scroll by nearly a screenful, C-l and z z recentre, and the mouse wheel scrolls the window it is over. The settings *scroll-margin* and *scroll-recenter* are described there too. They decide how the window follows the cursor when one of the moves in this chapter takes the cursor out of sight.

2.9 Counts

Most moves in this chapter take a count: you say how many times to do the move. Press C-u, type the number, and then press the key of the move. In NORMAL state, C-u 5 j moves five lines down. The echo area shows C-u 5- while Tuatara waits for the key.

C-u with no number means 4, and C-u C-u means 16. A minus sign after C-u makes the count negative, so C-u - 2 w moves back two words, as b (non-modal: M-b) would. A number you type after C-u C-u starts a new count of its own.

. in NORMAL state runs the last command again, with the count it had. Press . after C-u 3 j and the cursor goes three more lines down.

Other chapters use the same count for other things, such as how many times to insert a character. The count is always taken by the key that follows it.

Try it, with a long letter

  1. Open any file that has more than 30 lines. Press g g (non-modal: M-<) to go to the top.
  2. Press C-u 3 0 j. The cursor is on line 31.
  3. Press C-u C-u k. You have gone 16 lines up, to line 15.
  4. Press C-u - 2 w to move back two words.
  5. Press j (non-modal: C-n), then .. The last command, j, runs once more.

2.10 Marks and going back

The mark is a place that Tuatara remembers, and that stays where it is while the cursor moves on. The text between the mark and the cursor is the region, drawn in the :region face. The jumps in this chapter leave the mark where the cursor was, so a long jump can be taken back.

Each buffer keeps the marks it has had in a mark ring, which holds the last 16. Tuatara also keeps a global mark ring, with the marks of the buffers you set marks in.

Tuatara keeps a second list as well: the places the cursor was at before each move, ten for each buffer. S goes back to the place before the cursor's last move, and S again goes further back, round the list. It is meant for taking back a key that jumped to the wrong place. The echo area says Position 2 of 7 so you know how far back you are. A count before S skips that many places.

Registers and bookmarks keep a place for longer. A register (C-x r p, then a character) keeps the place until Tuatara quits, and C-x r j with the same character goes back to it, even after the text above it has changed. A bookmark (C-x m m, then a name) is kept for good, and C-x m b goes to one by its name.

Try it, with a long list

  1. Open a file of more than 30 lines. Press g g (non-modal: M-<), then : (non-modal: M-g g), type 30 and press RET. The echo area says Mark set and you are on line 30.
  2. Press g e (non-modal: M->) to go to the end. Press S. You are on the place before the jump, line 30. Press S again, and you go further back.
  3. Press C-u C-SPC. The cursor goes to the mark, line 30, where you pressed g e. Press C-u C-SPC once more and you are back at line 1.
  4. Press C-x r p, then a. Press g e. Press C-x r j, then a. The cursor goes back to where you set the register.
  5. Press C-SPC at line 1, then j j j, and the region is shown. Press C-x C-x. The cursor and the mark swap places. Press C-g to drop the region.

2.12 In help, lists and other read-only buffers

The windows that show help, the *Messages* log and similar read-only text are in what the mode line calls SUPPORT state. The reading keys of NORMAL state work there too: h, j, k, l, w, W, b, B, g g, g e, g h, g l, S, : and z z. They move the cursor and change nothing.

A list that a command made, such as the list of buffers or of the files in a folder, may use some of these letters for its own purposes. In a list h closes the window, and g starts a move, so the list is made again with r. The chapter for each list says what its keys do.
