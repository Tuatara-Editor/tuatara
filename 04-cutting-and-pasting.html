Cutting and pasting

How to move text from one place to another, and to other programs.

Cutting takes text out of the buffer and keeps it. Copying keeps text and leaves it where it is. Pasting puts the kept text in at the cursor. Emacs calls cutting "killing" and pasting "yanking", and Tuatara's command names use those words, so you will meet them here. This chapter shows the keys that cut, copy and paste in both states, where the cut text is kept, how it reaches other programs, how to keep several pieces of text at once and how to set lines aside so that one key takes them together.

Chapter 5 teaches selecting, rectangles and many cursors. Here a selection is only a way to say which text a key acts on.

4.1 The kill ring

Everything you cut or copy goes on the kill ring, a list of your latest cuts with the newest first. A paste puts the newest one in. Figure 4.1 shows the ring after three cuts.

The ring keeps *kill-ring-max* cuts. When it is full, the oldest is forgotten. Tuatara has no command that steps back through the older cuts, so a paste always gives you the newest. To keep a piece of text while you go on cutting and copying other things, put it in a register, described below.

Two cuts in a row, with no other command between them, join into one. Press C-k (modal: D) twice and the second cut is added to the first, so that one paste brings both back, in order.

A cut that is too large for the system clipboard (see *clipboard-size-limit* below) stays where it is in the buffer instead of being copied, and the echo area says so when you copy it. It stays good after you kill the buffer it came from. Text from an encrypted file never goes on the clipboard, and it leaves the ring when its buffer is closed.

A cut that would take part of the text a folded heading hides shows the fold instead. Press the same key again to cut.

4.2 Cutting, copying and pasting with the Emacs keys

These keys mean the same in NORMAL and INSERT state. They act on the region, the text between the cursor and the mark. A selection made with v is a region too. If you have not selected anything, C-SPC sets the mark at the cursor, and the region grows as you move away from it.

Try it, with a shopping list

  1. Press C-x C-f, type ~/shopping.txt and press RET. Press i, then type eggs, RET, bread, RET, milk, RET, butter.
  2. Press M-< (modal: g g) to go to the top. Press C-k (modal: D). The word eggs is cut and the line is empty. Press C-k again. The empty line goes, and bread is now on the first line.
  3. Press C-y. The cuts come back as one, eggs and its line end, in front of bread. Press C-/ (modal: u) to undo that.
  4. Press C-SPC, then C-n (modal: j). The region covers the first line. Press M-w. The echo area says Copied and nothing is removed.
  5. Press M-> (modal: g e) to go to the end, then C-y. The copied line is pasted after butter, and as that line has no line end the two run together. Press C-x C-s to save.

4.3 Cutting, copying and pasting in NORMAL state

In NORMAL state the letter keys do the same work on one key each. With a selection they act on it. Without one they act on the character under the cursor, or on as many characters as the number you gave first.

4.4 The system clipboard and the primary selection

The system clipboard is the one every program on your desktop shares. What you cut or copy in Tuatara goes there as well, so you can paste it in another program. When another program has copied something since, C-y and p paste that instead and put it first on the kill ring.

The primary selection is a second place that many Linux programs have. It holds whatever text was selected last, with no copy command. In Tuatara, a selection you make by dragging with the mouse becomes the primary selection when you let go. Clicking the middle mouse button in another program pastes it. In Tuatara, the middle button pastes the primary selection of any program at the place you clicked, with the mark left at its start. If nothing is selected anywhere, the echo area says Nothing is selected anywhere. A selection made with the keys does not become the primary selection.

The same limits apply to both. Text larger than *clipboard-size-limit* is not sent, and neither is any text from an encrypted file, and with *use-system-clipboard* set to nil nothing is sent or taken.

noneM-x gpg-copy-to-clipboardgpg-copy-to-clipboardPuts the text copied last on the system clipboard even when it came from an encrypted file. Other programs, a clipboard manager among them, can then read and keep it.

4.5 Registers

A register keeps one thing under one character, for as long as Tuatara runs. The character is a letter, a digit or a sign. A capital letter and a small letter name different registers. A register can hold text, a block of columns, a place in a file or a whole number. Keeping something in a register does not touch the kill ring, so a register is where to put what you want to paste later, however much you cut in between.

Every register command asks for its character with one key and no RET. C-g gives up. Keeping again under the same character replaces what was there.

Try it, with a recipe

  1. Press C-x C-f, type ~/pancakes.txt and press RET. Press i and type 2 eggs, 200 g flour, milk, RET, Mix, rest, fry., RET.
  2. Press M-< (modal: g g), C-SPC, C-e (modal: g l) to select the first line without its line end. Press C-x r s, then e. The echo area says Register e keeps text.
  3. Press C-n C-a C-k to cut the text of the second line. Press C-x r i, then e. The ingredients are written in at the cursor, although the kill ring holds the method.
  4. Press C-u 4 C-x r n, then n. Register n keeps the number 4. Press C-x r +, then n. It now keeps 5. Press C-x r i, then n to write 5 into the text.
  5. Press C-x r l. The list shows each register with what it keeps. Press q to close it.
ModalNon-modalCommandWhat it does
noneC-x r sregister-copyKeeps the selected text under a character, or the block of columns when a rectangle is selected.

4.6 Banked lines

Banking sets whole lines aside, wherever they are in the text, and shows them in a colour of their own. Then y, d or p takes all the banked lines at once, together with the lines of any selection you have made. This is the easy way to gather lines that are far apart. Banking works in NORMAL state.

You can bank a line, move away and bank another. Banked lines follow the text through edits, and they stay banked until you take them out of the bank or until y, d or p uses them. C-g leaves them as they are, and c does not act on them.

Try it, with a shopping list

  1. 1. Open ~/shopping.txt from the first try, or make a new file with six lines: eggs, bread, milk, apples, tea, butter. Press C-g so that you are in NORMAL state. 2. Press g g j to go to bread, then m l (non-modal: C-z m l). The line changes colour and the echo area says Banked this line (1 total), then what to do next. 3. Press j j j to go to tea, then m l again. Now two lines are banked. 4. Press m u on tea. It leaves the bank. Press m l to bank it again. 5. Press d. Both lines are cut together and leave the bank, and the echo area says Deleted 2 lines. 6. Press g g p. bread and tea are pasted above eggs, as one cut. Press C-x C-s to save.
ModalNon-modalCommandWhat it does
m lC-z m lbank-linesBanks the line, or the lines the selection touches, and drops the selection. On lines that are all banked already it takes them out again, and on lines partly banked it banks the rest. A number banks that many lines from the cursor. A line whose end a fold hides is banked with all that the fold hides. Lines a search filter hides are not banked.
m uC-z m ubank-release-lineTakes the line at the cursor out of the bank and keeps the others.

m DEL, m <delete>C-z m DEL, C-z m <delete>bank-clearLets go of every banked line in the buffer. The text is not changed.

With lines banked, y copies them as one cut and d cuts them as one cut and one change, so that one u (non-modal: C-/) puts them back. p puts the newest cut in place of them, as one change. Each of the three takes the banked lines out of the bank. A cut too large to paste over banked lines is refused, and the echo area gives its size.

4.7 Pasting into prompts

When Tuatara asks you a question in the minibuffer, you can paste the answer.

ModalNon-modalCommandWhat it does
noneC-y, C-V, S-<insert>minibuffer-pasteWrites in the system clipboard's text, without the line end after it. With nothing on the clipboard it writes the text cut last. A suggested answer that is highlighted is replaced.

C-V is Control, Shift and v together, the paste key of most terminals. Typing in a prompt does not touch the kill ring: C-k (modal: D) deletes the rest of the answer and C-u clears all of it, and neither keeps what it removed. A password prompt takes only what is on the clipboard, puts it in the answer and nowhere else, and never in the kill ring.

Try it, with a file name

  1. In another program, such as your file manager, copy a file name, for example shopping.txt.
  2. In Tuatara press C-x C-f. The prompt asks for a file.
  3. Press C-y. The name is written after the prompt. Press RET to open it.

In a terminal buffer, terminal-paste pastes into the program that runs there, and a middle click does the same with the primary selection (terminal-paste-primary). Chapter 17 describes both, with the setting *terminal-paste-keys*.

When you paste into the terminal that Tuatara itself runs in, with that terminal's own paste key, insert-pasted-text puts the text in at the cursor as text. None of it is taken for keys or commands. Its line ends become the buffer's, and one u (non-modal: C-/) takes it all back. It has no key.
