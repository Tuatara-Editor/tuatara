Tuatara tutor
==============

This is an ordinary, editable buffer, and the text you are reading is the
text you will practise on.  Changing it is the point -- nothing here is
precious, and \[tuatara-tutor] after killing this buffer gives you a fresh
copy whenever you want one.

Lines beginning with ">>" are things to DO.  Everything else is
explanation.

Tuatara's modal editing has two states.  In NORMAL state the letter keys
are commands; in INSERT state they type.  The mode line shows which:
TUATARA[N] or TUATARA[I].  If a key ever does something you did not
expect, you are probably in the other state -- press \`C-g' to get back to
NORMAL.

\`C-g' also cancels a selection, and it is worth pressing at the end of
any lesson that made one.  A selection left active changes what the next
lesson's keys do: several of them act on the selection when there is one
and on the character or line at point when there is not.

To stop, kill this buffer with \`C-x k'.


Lesson 1 -- moving around
-------------------------

Movement sits on the home row:

    \[backward-char] left    \[next-line] down    \[previous-line] up    \[forward-char] right

>> Walk the cursor down to the ----> line, along it, and back, using
   only those four keys.

   ----> Move along this line and back again before going on.

Bigger jumps:

    \[forward-word] next word     \[backward-word] previous word
    \[beginning-of-buffer] buffer start   \[end-of-buffer] buffer end

>> Press \[beginning-of-buffer] to jump to the top of this buffer, then come back here.

Big jumps are easy to get wrong, so they are cheap to undo.  \[jump-back]
returns to where you were before the last one -- reach for \`g l' to get to
the end of the LINE, slip and hit \`g e' instead, and you are at the end of
the BUFFER.  One keystroke puts it right.

>> Press \[end-of-buffer] to shoot to the end of this buffer, then \[jump-back] to come
   straight back to this line.

Press it again to keep walking back through earlier positions.  It is a
recovery key rather than a filing system.


Lesson 2 -- counts
------------------

A count works wherever "how many" means something, and it always means
exactly that.  Give it as \`C-u' N before the key.

Every motion takes one, and so does every \`m' key that selects a thing,
along with \[delete-selection-or-char], \[copy-selection-or-char], \[tuatara-change], \[yank-over-selection], \[tuatara-open-below], \[tuatara-open-above] and \[select-lines].  The keys with no
"how many" in them -- entering INSERT at the cursor, the other two
selection toggles, asking for help -- ignore a count rather than
refusing it, so a guess there costs nothing.

Mind the \`C-u': a count always starts with it.  A bare \`3' does NOT start a
count here -- digits are unbound in NORMAL state.  Worse than doing
nothing, \`3' \[next-line] says "3 is undefined" and then runs the \[next-line] on its
own, so you move ONE line instead of three.  The count is dropped, not
obeyed.  \`C-u 3' \[next-line] is how it is said.

>> Press \`C-u 3' \[next-line] and watch the cursor move three lines in one go.

>> Put the cursor on "one" below and press \`C-u 5' \[forward-word] -- five words in
   one go, leaving the cursor just after "five".  \[forward-word] lands at the END
   of each word, so counting stops there rather than on the next one.

   ----> one two three four five six seven eight nine ten

Counts work the same way on the editing commands you are about to meet.
Lessons 4 and 5 each end with a line to try one on, once the command
itself has been introduced -- a count is easier to see when you already
know what it is counting.

Where the cursor sits matters more for some commands than others, and it
is worth knowing which is which before you start counting:

  - Counting CHARACTERS counts from the cursor.  Put it on the first
    character you mean to affect; one place off and you act on the wrong
    five.
  - Counting or selecting THINGS -- a word, a sentence, a paragraph --
    does not care where inside the thing you are.  Anywhere in the word
    is anywhere in the word.

The second kind is why the exercises for it say "anywhere", and the
first kind is why the others name an exact character.


Lesson 3 -- typing
------------------

To type, enter INSERT state.  Modal editing then gets out of the way, and the
keys do what they do without it: letters type themselves.

    \[tuatara-insert] before the cursor      \[tuatara-insert-after] after the cursor
    \[tuatara-insert-line-start] at the start of the line   \[tuatara-insert-line-end] at the end of the line
    \[tuatara-open-below] open a line below       \[tuatara-open-above] open a line above

Both open keys take a count: \`C-u 3' \[tuatara-open-below] opens three lines below and
leaves you on the first of them, with two empty lines under it.

A character that is not on your keyboard has a code of its own, and
\[list-digraphs] lists them all; Lesson 13 is the short way to type
one.

>> Put the cursor on the full stop below, press \[tuatara-insert], type the missing
   word -- it is "dog" -- then press \`C-g' to return to NORMAL.

   ----> The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy .


Lesson 4 -- deleting and changing
---------------------------------

    \[delete-selection-or-char]   delete the character under the cursor, or the selection
    \[tuatara-change]   change it -- deletes, then drops you into INSERT

>> Fix the doubled letters on the ----> line using \[delete-selection-or-char].

   ----> Thiis liine haas extraa letterss in itt.

>> Put the cursor on the "w" of "wrong" below -- the FIRST character, not
   just somewhere in the word -- then press \`C-u 5' \[tuatara-change], type "right",
   and press \`C-g'.

   ----> This word is wrong and needs replacing.

   One character off and you replace the wrong five: starting on the
   "r" gives "wrightand".  Lesson 5 shows the way round that.

Both take a count, the same one Lesson 2 described.

>> Put the cursor on the first "x" below and press \`C-u 3' \[delete-selection-or-char].
   All three go at once.

   ----> xxxand the rest of the line stays


Lesson 5 -- selecting things
----------------------------

\[start-selection] drops a mark and starts a selection that grows as you move: press
it, move, and everything between is selected.  \`C-g' lets go.

Pressing \[start-selection] a second time does not let go.  It drops a fresh mark
where you are standing and starts a new selection from there, so the
one you had is gone but you are still selecting.

>> Put the cursor at the start of the ----> line, press \[start-selection], then move
   right with \[forward-char] and down with \[next-line].  Press \`C-g' to drop the
   selection.

   ----> Select part of this line by hand before meeting the shortcuts.

That is the manual way.  Usually it is quicker to select the thing you
mean:

    \[select-word] a word        \[select-symbol] a symbol
    \[select-sentence] a sentence    \[select-paragraph] a paragraph
    \[select-whole-buffer] the whole buffer

>> Put the cursor anywhere in the first ----> sentence and press \[select-sentence].  The
   whole sentence is selected, however long it is.  Press \[select-sentence] again:
   the selection GROWS to take the second sentence as well.

   ----> Selecting by meaning beats counting characters.  It also reads better.

All four of these grow on a second press, and keep growing until the
buffer runs out.  A count says the same thing in one go, so \[select-word] \[select-word]
and \`C-u 2' \[select-word] agree.  Any other key in between ends the run, and
the next press starts a fresh selection.

All four can grow BACKWARD too: \[select-word-backward] adds the word before the
selection, \[select-symbol-backward] the symbol, \[select-sentence-backward] the sentence, \[select-paragraph-backward] the paragraph.
Either direction continues the same run, so \[select-word] \[select-word] \[select-word-backward] takes
two words forward and then one back.  Pressed fresh on a word, each
selects the same word its forward partner would; pressed in the gap
between two, \[select-word] takes the word ahead and \[select-word-backward] the one behind, so
from one space the two keys are the two words on either side of it.
Runs mix objects, too: each press adds one object of its own kind at
its own end, so \[select-word] \[select-sentence] grows the word selection forward to the
end of its sentence.

\[mark-run-toggle] holds the prefix down for you.  In mark run mode the bare letters
w W b B s S mark and grow exactly as their m-prefixed keys do, so \`M' \`w' \`b'
selects what three prefixed presses select, the press itself being
the first of them.  A reminder of the object keys stays in the echo
area for as long as the mode is on, and goes when the mode does -- as
one does for v, V and m v too, each naming what its own selection
answers to.  It names the keys whose SUBJECT the mode changes and no
others -- w moves by a word in NORMAL state and marks one here --
while a key that keeps its subject is left out: h j k l still move,
J and K still work on lines, \`.' still repeats, u and U still step
back and forward.  All of them are below.

The press arrives holding a word, that being what nearly every run
starts from -- so \`M' alone is a selected word, and \`M' \[delete-selection-or-char] takes it.
Which word is m w's answer exactly: the one under the cursor, or from
a gap the one ahead of it, so the two keys never disagree.  The word is
the run's first press, and every letter after it grows: \`M' is m w,
and \`M' \`w' is two words.

>> Put the cursor on "three" in the ----> line and press \[mark-run-toggle]: one
   word is selected.  Press \`w': two words, no prefix in sight.  Press
   \`b' and the word before joins them.  Now press \[delete-selection-or-char] to take all
   three.

   ----> one two three four five six

J and K grow the selection by lines, J at the bottom end and K at the
top, each finishing its own end's line first: from a word, \`J' reaches
the end of its line, newline and all, \`K' the start, and \`J' \`K' is the
whole line -- so a delete after the pair removes it outright, and
from a line's first word \`J' alone is enough.  h j k l move point and
adjust the selection's near end; g h and g l are the two that own an
end apiece instead, so they add up -- \`M' \`g' \`h' \`g' \`l' takes the whole
line's text, in either order.  * trades which end the motions hold;
the object keys trade it back before they grow, so they never need
thinking about.

\`u' puts the run back where the last press found it and \`U' puts it
forward again, one press per step, the motions and * included.  A run
only ever grows, so without them a press that reached further than it
looked left cancelling and starting again as the only way out.
Outside the mode those two keys are undo and redo, and inside it they
are the same idea one level down -- of the run rather than the buffer.
\`.' repeats the last press, as it does everywhere: \`M' \`w' \`.' is three
words, and each \`.' is one step for \`u' to take back.

>> Press \[mark-run-toggle] on "three" in the ----> line above, then
   \`w' \`w' \`w': four words.  Too many?  Press \`u' twice to take two
   of them back, and \`U' once if you went one step too far.

Paragraphs keep their m prefix there, since p and P stay the paste
keys inside the mode: m p and m P grow a run just as the bare letters
do.  A selection you already have -- from \[start-selection], \[select-lines] or the mark keys -- is
ADOPTED by \[mark-run-toggle] rather than dropped, and the object keys grow it from there.

Any other key returns to NORMAL state and does its ordinary job in the
same press, so \[mark-run-toggle] \`w' \`w' \[delete-selection-or-char] selects two words and deletes them with
no explicit exit.  Two presses are held back from that: a key that
does nothing -- one that is unbound -- leaves the run standing, so a
mistyped key costs nothing but a message, and \[select-lines] says to
leave the run first rather than quietly taking it over.  \[mark-run-toggle] again --
or \`C-g' -- drops the selection and the mode with it.

A word stops at a hyphen or underscore; a symbol runs straight through
one.  On a name held together by them the two select very different
things, which is why both keys exist.

>> Put the cursor on the "m" of "mail" in the ----> line and press \[select-word]:
   only that one word is selected.  Press \`C-g', then press \[select-symbol]
   from the same spot: the whole name is selected, hyphen, underscore
   and all.

   ----> Call send-mail_to when the queue drains.

In Lisp, every character a Lisp name can hold is part of a symbol, so
\[select-symbol] on "foo.bar" there takes the whole of it, and a quote in front
comes too.  A full stop or comma at the END is always left out, so
\[select-symbol] on the last name in a sentence gives you the name without the
full stop.

A selection is something to act ON, so the editing keys from Lesson 4
follow straight on from it: \[tuatara-change] changes what is selected rather than the
character under the cursor.

>> Put the cursor on "replace" below, press \[select-word] then \[tuatara-change], type
   "change", and press \`C-g'.  Selecting first means you never count
   the characters.

   ----> Words to replace without counting anything.

These take a count too -- the same one Lesson 2 described.

>> Put the cursor on "alpha" below and press \`C-u 2' \[select-word].  Two words
   are selected instead of one.

   ----> alpha beta gamma delta

You can also select by delimiter.  \[select-inside-pair] asks for a character and selects
what is INSIDE the nearest pair; \[select-pair] includes the delimiters too.

>> Put the cursor between the parentheses below and press \[select-inside-pair] then \`(' --
   the text inside is selected.  Try \[select-pair] then \`(' to include the brackets.

   ----> call(this argument here)

Standing ON a delimiter, either end of the pair, nothing is asked: the
character under the cursor is the answer, so \[select-inside-pair] alone is enough
there.  A delimiter typed after it is a key of its own and acts at
once: it wraps what is selected, or takes that pair off again.

>> Put the cursor on the "(" of the ----> line above and press \[select-inside-pair] by
   itself.  The same text is selected, with no question asked.

>> Press \`(' now.  The parentheses come off, since they are the pair
   around what you selected.  Press \`(' once more to put them back.

A count means levels out, so \`C-u 2' \[select-inside-pair] \`(' from the inner pair selects the
outer one.

>> Put the cursor on "deep" below and press \`C-u 2' \[select-inside-pair] then \`('.

   ----> outer (middle (deep) middle) outer

The pairs \[select-inside-pair] and \[select-pair] know are a FIXED list: the brackets, quotes
straight and curly, and a row of markup characters such as * = ~ and
_.  They look for the character itself and nothing else, wherever it
stands -- a "<" is a pair to them in plain text as much as in HTML,
and a bracket inside a string or a comment counts the same as one
outside it.

The list is yours to change.  *pair-delimiters* holds the pairs,
and adding # to it in your init file makes # a delimiter too -- any
character you like, X included.  \[describe-variable] on it shows the pairs in
force, and the init file Tuatara writes for you shows the line an
addition takes.

\[select-sexp-inside] and \[select-sexp] do the same job without asking.  They find the enclosing
brackets themselves, whatever kind those turn out to be -- useful in
code, where the nearest pair is as likely to be square or curly as
round.  In a language with a grammar, a bracket inside a string or a
comment is no bracket at all to them.

    \[select-inside-pair] and \[select-pair]   you name the delimiter, from a fixed list
    \[select-sexp-inside] and \[select-sexp]   Tuatara finds the brackets itself

>> Put the cursor on the "2" below and press \[select-sexp-inside].  "1 2 3" is selected
   without you naming the bracket.  Press \[select-sexp] instead and the square
   brackets come with it.

   ----> (defun f (a b) [1 2 3])

Counts go outward here too, and cross bracket types on the way out.

>> From that same "2", press \`C-u 2' \[select-sexp-inside].  The selection jumps past the
   square brackets to what is inside the surrounding parentheses.


Lesson 6 -- whole lines
-----------------------

\[select-lines] starts a line selection anchored on the current line.  \[select-next-line] and
\[select-previous-line] then grow it a whole line at a time, and they take counts too.
So does \[select-lines] itself: \`C-u 3' \[select-lines] selects three lines in one press.

>> Put the cursor on the first ----> line, press \[select-lines], then \[select-next-line] twice, then \[delete-selection-or-char].
   All three lines go, leaving no blank behind.

   ----> first line to remove
   ----> second line to remove
   ----> third line to remove

The highlight stops at the end of the last line, so the newline that ends
it never LOOKS selected -- but \[copy-selection-or-char] and \[delete-selection-or-char] take it anyway.  That
is why the three lines above go completely, instead of leaving three
empty ones behind, and why a \[copy-selection-or-char] here pastes back as whole lines rather
than running into whatever line it lands on.  Worth knowing before you
report it: the selection is one character shorter than what it takes.

Cutting and copying with \[kill-region] and \[kill-ring-save] takes only what is highlighted,
the last newline left out, so for whole lines use the modal keys.

Lines can be put back together as well as taken apart.  \[join-selected-lines] pulls the
line BELOW up onto the one you are on, tidying the whitespace at the
join -- the direction you want when you are sitting on a line deciding
to absorb what follows.

>> Put the cursor on the first ----> line below and press \[join-selected-lines].  The
   second line joins it.  Press it again and the third comes up too.

   ----> a sentence broken
   ----> across three
   ----> separate lines

A count joins that many lines at once, so \`C-u 2' \[join-selected-lines] from the first
line would have done both in one go.

Selected lines join as one.  \[select-lines] \[select-next-line] \[select-next-line] \[join-selected-lines] on the three lines above
would have made the one line too, and dropped the selection.


Lesson 7 -- copy and paste
--------------------------

    \[copy-selection-or-char] copy    \[delete-selection-or-char] cut    \[yank-over-selection] paste    \[yank-rectangle] paste a rectangle

What you copy or cut is on the system clipboard too, so another program
can paste it, and what another program copied is what \[yank-over-selection] pastes next.

A count on \[yank-over-selection] pastes that many copies.

>> Copy the word "echo" below with \[select-word] then \[copy-selection-or-char], then press \`C-u 3' \[yank-over-selection] at the
   end of the line.

   ----> echo

Pasting REPLACES whatever is selected, rather than inserting alongside
it.  That is worth knowing before the next lesson, where it is how a
whole set of banked lines gets swapped in one press.


Lesson 8 -- banking, which was donkey.el's own idea
-----------------------------------------------

Most editors make you copy one stretch at a time.  Tuatara lets you set
aside lines from anywhere in the buffer and act on all of them at once.

    \[bank-selected-lines] bank this line, or every line a selection touches
        (press it again on a banked line to take it back)
        (\`C-u 3' \[bank-selected-lines] banks three lines, as selecting them would)
    \[unbank-line] unbank this line     \[unbank-section] unbank the whole run
    \[clear-banked-lines] discard every bank

Banked lines stay highlighted while you carry on moving around.  When you
press \[copy-selection-or-char] or \[delete-selection-or-char], every banked line is taken at once, as a single
piece, in the order they appear in the buffer -- and \[yank-over-selection] swaps the whole set
in for whatever you copied.

>> Bank the first and third shopping lines below with \[bank-selected-lines], then press \[copy-selection-or-char].
   The message reads "Copied 2 lines".  Now put the cursor on the
   (paste here) line, press \[select-lines] to select it, and press \[yank-over-selection]: both
   banked lines arrive at once, and the line you had selected is gone.

   The \[select-lines] is what makes the marker line disappear: pasting replaces a
   selection, as Lesson 7 showed.  Without it the two lines would simply
   have been inserted, leaving the marker sitting underneath them.

   ----> milk
   ----> nails
   ----> bread

   (paste here)

Whatever is selected right now counts as well, so the last piece never has
to be banked explicitly.

>> Bank the "milk" line again, then select the "bread" line with \[start-selection] and
   \[forward-char], and press \[copy-selection-or-char].  Both are copied, though only one was banked.


Lesson 9 -- columns
-------------------

\[rectangle-mark-mode] makes the selection a RECTANGLE.  Instead of a run of text it
covers the same columns on every line it spans -- for editing a column of
a table, or the leading characters of a block of lines.

    \[rectangle-mark-mode] start a rectangle selection (press it again to cancel)

>> Put the cursor on the first "1" below, press \[rectangle-mark-mode], then \[next-line] twice and
   \[forward-char] twice.  Only the block of digits is highlighted, not the
   words.  Press \[delete-selection-or-char] to cut it out.

   Count the presses off the highlight rather than off the characters:
   the anchor column counts as one, so reaching the third digit takes two
   presses and not three.

   ----> 111 alpha
   ----> 222 beta
   ----> 333 gamma

The selection is released by the cut, so you can put the cursor back on
the first line and press \[yank-rectangle] straight away -- the block goes back where
it came from.  A rectangle has its own paste key: \[yank-over-selection] pastes ordinary
text, \[yank-rectangle] pastes columns, and neither has to guess which you meant.

\[tuatara-change] on a rectangle empties the block on EVERY line it spans and puts
a cursor on each, in INSERT state: what you type appears on every row
at once, the cursors of Lesson 16.  \`C-g' comes back to the cursors,
and \`C-g' again ends them.

>> Put the cursor on the first "7" below, press \[rectangle-mark-mode], then \[next-line] twice
   and \[forward-char] twice.  Press \[tuatara-change], type "##" and press \`C-g' twice.
   All three rows lose their digits together.

   ----> 777 red
   ----> 888 green
   ----> 999 blue

\[rectangle-mark-mode] on its own selects one CHARACTER on one line, so pressing \[tuatara-change]
right after it changes that single character -- correct, but rarely
what you wanted.  \[next-line] gives the block its rows and \[forward-char] its width, and
both have to happen before \[tuatara-change].

>> Put the cursor on the first "5" below, press \[rectangle-mark-mode], and press \[tuatara-change]
   immediately -- no \[next-line], no \[forward-char].  One character goes, and you
   type in its place as \[tuatara-change] always does; \`C-g' once is enough.  That is
   the whole difference between the two exercises.

   ----> 555 solo

The block does not have to cover any text at all.  A rectangle with NO
width empties nothing, so what you type is inserted, which is how the
same text goes at the front, or the end, of a run of lines at once.

\[rectangle-mark-mode] always starts one character wide, so for a prefix take that
width straight back off with \[backward-char] before going down.

>> Put the cursor on the "r" of "red" below, press \[rectangle-mark-mode] then \[backward-char],
   then \[next-line] twice.  Press \[tuatara-change], type "// " and press \`C-g' twice.
   Nothing is replaced; every row simply gains a front.

   ----> red
   ----> green
   ----> blue

For a suffix, start from the end of the line instead.  There is nothing
to the right to widen into, so the rectangle is already zero-width and
\[backward-char] is not wanted.

>> Put the cursor on the first row below and press \[end-of-line], then
   \[rectangle-mark-mode], then \[next-line] twice.  Press \[tuatara-change], type " ;" and press
   \`C-g' twice.

   ----> aaaaa
   ----> bbbbb
   ----> ccccc

A suffix lands on a COLUMN, though, not at the end of each line -- the
column the FIRST row happened to end on.  On rows of equal length, as
above, those are the same place.  On ragged rows they are not: a short
row is padded out to the column, and a long one is split at it.  The
corner of a block can stand past the end of a short line, and \[forward-char] moves
it on past the end of one rather than onto the next line.

A rectangle's columns are characters: a tab counts as one, as a letter
does.

Two things worth knowing before you rely on it:

  - A rectangle lives in Tuatara's own store, NOT the system clipboard.
    \[yank-rectangle] puts it back inside Tuatara; another program will paste
    whatever was on the clipboard before.  A block of columns has no
    shape a flat clipboard could carry.
  - Pasting a rectangle ONTO a rectangle needs the same number of rows.
    A three-row block over a two-row selection is refused with a message
    rather than half-applied.


Lesson 10 -- changing your mind
-------------------------------

Four keys start a selection: \`v', \`V', \[mark-run-toggle] and \`m' \`v'.  You can go from any one
to any other without letting go first, and what becomes of the
selection you already had is the whole of what there is to know.

    \[mark-run-toggle]     ADOPTS it.  It becomes the run's starting selection,
          and the object keys grow it from there.
    \`m' \`v'   REINTERPRETS it.  The region's corners become the block.
    \`V'     starts FRESH, anchored on the line the cursor is on.
    \`v'     RE-ANCHORS.  What you had is dropped and a new selection
          starts, empty, where the cursor stands.

>> Put the cursor on "beta" in the ----> line and press \`v', then \`l' three
   times, which selects "bet".  Now press \[mark-run-toggle] and then \`w': the run
   ADOPTED those three characters and grew them to the end of the word
   they were part of.

   ----> alpha beta gamma delta

Three exceptions are worth knowing.

Inside a mark run, \`v' and \`V' are REFUSED.  Neither can take a run's
selection over honestly, so Tuatara says how to let go instead:
\[mark-run-toggle], \`C-g', or any action key.

>> With that run still up, press \`v'.  The selection does not move,
   and the echo area tells you how to leave.  Press \[mark-run-toggle] to cancel.

A rectangle is the one selection \[mark-run-toggle] will not adopt: a block has no
forward end for the object keys to own.  So it is dropped, and a fresh
run starts on the word under the cursor.

>> Draw a rectangle with \`m' \`v' \`j' \`l', then press \[mark-run-toggle].  The block
   is gone and a single word is selected in its place.

   ----> one two three
   ----> four five six

And coming to \`m' \`v' FROM \`V' gives a FULL-WIDTH block.  \`V' selects
whole lines, so the corners the rectangle inherits are the whole line's
width -- not the narrow block you were drawing.  Start \`m' \`v' from
nothing, or from a \`v' selection, when you want a narrow one.

The \`m' keys end nothing.  \`m' \`w' inside a run grows the run, and
outside one it marks and leaves you selecting.  Over a rectangle it
clears the block first, no mark command being able to act on columns.
\`%' is the same: inside a run it takes the whole buffer, and the run
carries on.

Pressing a starter twice cancels it -- \`V' \`V', \`m' \`v' \`m' \`v',
\[mark-run-toggle] \[mark-run-toggle] -- except \`v', which re-anchors instead.


Lesson 11 -- when two selections disagree
-----------------------------------------

Banked lines are whole lines.  A rectangle is columns.  No command can act
on both at once, so Tuatara has one rule:

    THE SELECTION YOU ARE LOOKING AT WINS.  THE BANK IS THE FALLBACK.

    with a rectangle drawn    \[copy-selection-or-char] and \[delete-selection-or-char] take the rectangle; banks stay
    with no rectangle drawn   \[copy-selection-or-char] and \[delete-selection-or-char] take the banked lines
    with lines banked         \[yank-over-selection] replaces the banked lines

The rule is only needed for TAKING, because only \[copy-selection-or-char] and
\[delete-selection-or-char] can be pointed at either kind of selection.  Pasting
needs no rule: \[yank-over-selection] is text and \[yank-rectangle] is columns, and
they read from different stores.

First give \[yank-over-selection] something to paste.  A rectangle never reaches the kill
ring, so a rectangle copy on its own leaves \[yank-over-selection] with nothing to insert.

>> Put the cursor on the "col two" line below and press \[select-lines] then \[copy-selection-or-char].
   That is an ordinary whole-line copy, on the kill ring where \[yank-over-selection] looks.

   ----> keep this banked
   ----> col one
   ----> col two

>> Now bank the "keep" line with \[bank-selected-lines], draw a rectangle over the first
   three characters of both "col" lines, and press \[copy-selection-or-char].  The rectangle
   is copied -- and the "keep" line is STILL highlighted.  Nothing was
   spent.

   Copying a rectangle also drops it, the way copying any selection
   does, so there is no rectangle on screen by the time you press the
   next key.  Nothing has to be dismissed first.

>> Press \[yank-over-selection].  The banked line is replaced by the copied line: the
   bank was the selection still on screen, so it won.

>> Press \[yank-rectangle].  The rectangle lands, exactly as it would have
   before that paste, or an hour later.  It is in its own store, and
   nothing you do to the kill ring reaches it.

Neither store is ever emptied by work on the other.  Take the rectangle
and your banks are still waiting; take the banks and the rectangle is
still in its store.

Acting on banked lines does spend them: \[copy-selection-or-char], \[delete-selection-or-char] and
\[yank-over-selection] each empty the bank as they use it, the way any selection is
consumed by the command that acts on it.  \[clear-banked-lines] is
the one key that clears banks WITHOUT using them, and
\[unbank-line] drops a single line.


Lesson 12 -- wrapping a selection
---------------------------------

Select something and press a delimiter: it goes around the selection.
The selection is then dropped, so there is nothing left to cancel --
and that is the one thing to know about taking a pair off again.
Select the same text once more and press the same key: this time it
comes off.  One key, both directions, and what you have selected says
which of the two you get.

>> Put the cursor on "middle" in the ----> line, press \`m' \`w' to select
   it, then press \`('.  Now press \`m' \`w' \`(' again and the parentheses
   come off.

   ----> one middle three

Either half of a pair does the same thing, so \`)' wraps as \`(' does and
you can reach for whichever is nearer.  The delimiters are the pairs
\[select-inside-pair] and \[select-pair] already know -- the brackets, the quotes, the curved
quotes, the guillemets, and \`=' \`*' \`/' \`~' \`|' \`\' \`+' \`_' \`$' -- so whatever those
two can select, a key can wrap.

Two of them are not wrap keys: \`:' goes to a line and \`>' indents, and
they go on doing that.  \`<' still wraps, and gives you the
pair.

Nothing between the two characters is touched.  No backslashes are
added, in any mode: \[select-pair] \`"' then \`"' gives a plain pair around the pair,
and selecting what is inside takes one off again.

>> Put the cursor inside the quotes below and press \[select-inside-pair] \`"' -- the
   word is selected without the quotes.  Press \`"' and they come off.

   ----> she said "probably" and left

After \[select-inside-pair] or \[select-pair] the selection is still live, so the delimiter you
type next acts at once, with no second press and nothing to select
again: \[select-inside-pair] \`"' picks what the quotes hold, and the \`"' after it takes them
off.  Standing ON a delimiter, \[select-inside-pair] alone is enough -- the character
under the cursor is the answer.

With NOTHING selected these keys do nothing at all, which is the state
they spend most of their time in.

Under \[rectangle-mark-mode] each line of the block is wrapped at its own columns, and a
rectangle is never unwrapped: the pair goes on, row by row.

>> Put the cursor on the "a" of "alpha", press \[rectangle-mark-mode], then \`j' and
   \`l' \`l', and press \`['.  Each row is wrapped where the block stood.

   ----> alpha one
   ----> bravo two


Lesson 13 -- characters your keyboard does not have
---------------------------------------------------

\[insert-digraph] asks for two keys and inserts one character, with nothing
turned on and nothing to turn off afterwards.  It works from NORMAL
state, and a count repeats the character.

>> Put the cursor at the end of the ----> line and press \[insert-digraph], then
   \`E' \`u'.  A euro sign appears.  Press \`C-u' \`3' \[insert-digraph] \`-' \`M' for three
   em dashes.

   ----> the price is

The two keys are the character's digraph in RFC 1345, a standard list.
\[list-digraphs] lists every one of them with what it types, the common
ones at the top of the chart.

Now the part worth remembering.  With a SELECTION live, \[insert-digraph] WRAPS
in the character it names instead of inserting it -- which is the only
way to wrap in a character no key can type.  The closing half is
looked up the way a wrap key looks it up, so a pair stays a pair.

>> Put the cursor on "quoted" below, press \`m' \`w', then \[insert-digraph] and
   \`<' \`<'.  The word is wrapped in guillemets.  Press \`m' \`w' and
   \[insert-digraph] \`"' \`6' for curved double quotes instead.

   ----> make this quoted please

That is the whole of it: two keys for one character, a selection first
if you want it wrapped.  To type MANY of them, turn digraphs on with
\[digraph-input-mode] and in INSERT state type \`&' and the two keys as you go;
\[input-method-off] turns them off again, \[toggle-input-method] off and on.


Lesson 14 -- editing every match at once
----------------------------------------

\[split-selection] asks for a regexp and holds every match in what you have
selected.  It changes nothing by itself.  It selects, and then waits
for you to say what to do with all of them at once:

  \`i' types before every match, \`a' after it, \`c' empties them first,
  \`d' deletes them, \`w' or any delimiter wraps them, and \`C-g' ends it.

A painted match is ONE thing, the way a single character is: \`a' types
after the whole of it and \`i' before the whole of it, wherever the
cursor sits.  That is not how a \`v' selection behaves: there \`a'
types after the character under the cursor and \`i' before it,
wherever in the selection the cursor is.

While you type, what you write appears at every match together.
Erase with \`DEL', the Backspace key, and retype as you like: it is
ordinary INSERT state, and \`C-g' ends it KEEPING what you typed,
exactly as it does everywhere else.  A \`DEL' just before the
matches, or a \`C-d' just after them, is made at every one of them
too.  Once the split has ended, \`u' takes back everything it wrote,
at every match, in one step.

>> Put the cursor on the first price line below and press \`v' \`j' \`j'
   \[end-of-line] to select all three.  Press \[split-selection], type price and
   \`RET'.  Press \`i' and type unit_ -- every price becomes unit_price as
   you type.  Press \`C-g' when you are done.

   ----> total = price + tax
   ----> total = price - discount
   ----> total = price * rate

>> Select the same three lines, press \[split-selection], type total and \`RET',
   then \`a' and type _cost.  \`C-g'.

>> Once more: \[split-selection], = and \`RET', then \`c' and type :=.  Every =
   changes at once.  \`C-g'.

What \`c' and \`d' remove reaches the kill ring as ONE kill: a single
copy when the matches agree, so \[yank-over-selection] pastes what was there rather
than a column of copies, and every match on a line of its own when they
differ.

>> Select the three DRAFT lines, press \[split-selection], type DRAFT: and \`SPC'
   and \`RET', then \`d'.  The label leaves all three lines at once.

   ----> DRAFT: open the window
   ----> DRAFT: close the door
   ----> DRAFT: feed the cat

A wrap is the one verb that leaves the split standing: the matches keep
their highlight and the reminder still offers every verb.  So a second
pair goes inside the first, the same pair again takes it off, and any
other verb can follow.

>> Select the three apple lines, press \[split-selection], type apple and \`RET',
   then press \`(' -- every apple is (apple), and the split is still
   there.  Press \`[' for ([apple]), and \`[' again to take the brackets
   off.  Now press \`a' and type s: (apples).  \`C-g'.

   ----> apple pie
   ----> apple juice
   ----> apple tree

\`w' does the same with a pair it asks you to name: \`w' then \`"'
wraps every match in quotes.

A match can be empty, which is how you reach the end of every line:
\`$' holds a place at each line end whatever the line holds, and \`^'
holds one at each start.

>> Select the three lines below, press \[split-selection], type $ and \`RET', then
   \`a' and \`;'.  Every line gains a semicolon, ragged right edge and
   all.

   ----> int a = 1
   ----> long bb = 22
   ----> char ccc = 333


Lesson 15 -- choosing what a split holds
----------------------------------------

What a split searches is what you selected, and no more.  With nothing
selected it is the line the cursor is on -- there is no whole-buffer
default, because \[select-whole-buffer] makes the buffer a selection like any other
and that way you reach it by choosing it.

>> Put the cursor on the first date below, press \[split-selection], type / and
   \`RET', then \`c' and \`-'.  Only that line's slashes change.  \`C-g'.

   ----> 2026/09/24
   ----> 2026/09/25

Or type the regexp with % in front, and the whole buffer is searched,
whatever is selected; the % is not part of the search, and \% at the
start searches for a percent sign.

>> Press \[split-selection], type %split and \`RET'.  The reminder counts every split
   in this buffer, not only this line's.  \`C-g' lets them go.

Under \[rectangle-mark-mode] the search stays INSIDE the block; a count before
\[split-selection] widens it to each row's whole line.

Banked lines, from Lesson 8, reach lines that are not next to each
other.  Every banked line is searched, and opening the split spends the
bank, as \[copy-selection-or-char] and \[delete-selection-or-char] do.

>> Bank the two keep lines below with \[bank-selected-lines] on each, leaving the
   skip lines alone.  Press \[split-selection], type : and \`RET', then \`c' and
   type \`SPC' and =.  Only the banked lines change, and the bank is gone.
   \`C-g'.

   ----> keep: red
   ----> skip: green
   ----> keep: blue
   ----> skip: yellow

A \`v' selection made after banking is searched along with the bank,
exactly as it is selected -- the part you chose, not its whole line.

>> Bank the first pear line.  On the third, put the cursor on the
   second pear and press \`v' \[end-of-line].  Now press \[split-selection], type pear
   and \`RET', then \`c' and type fig: both pears on the banked line
   change, and on the third only the one you selected.  \`C-g'.

   ----> pear, apple, pear
   ----> pear, apple, pear
   ----> pear, apple, pear

The regexp is the one \[replace-regexp] and \`C-s' read.  Case is ignored while it
holds no capital letter, and a capital makes it exact.

>> Select the three lines below, press \[split-selection], type todo and \`RET',
   then \`i' and type [x] and \`SPC'.  All three are marked, whatever their
   case.  \`C-g', \`u' to take the marks back, and try Todo instead:
   only the line written that way is held.

   ----> TODO fix the door
   ----> Todo paint the fence
   ----> todo wash the car

The matches do not have to agree.

>> Select the three item lines, press \[split-selection], type [0-9]+ and \`RET',
   and press \`(': every number is wrapped, whatever its length.  Then
   \`a' and type \`SPC' and pcs, for (7 pcs), (42 pcs) and (365 pcs).
   \`C-g'.

   ----> item 7
   ----> item 42
   ----> item 365

Where the matches differ, what you type goes at their edges: moving
into one ends the split, since there is no one text to edit in all of
them.  And matches that touch are refused -- a over aaa -- since text
typed where two meet would belong to both.

In a regexp some characters are special: \d is a digit, \s a
blank, \w a letter or digit, (a|b) is a or b, and x{3} is three x.  A
bracket, a brace, a dot, a star or a plus that is meant as itself takes
a backslash first: \. is a full stop.

Some things come with their whole pattern ready, as their standards
define them, a name in double brackets: [[:ipv4:]] is an IP address,
[[:email:]] an e-mail address, [[:creditcard:]] a card number whose check
digit is right, [[:iban:]] a bank account's IBAN.  They work wherever a
regexp is typed.

Dates are compared as dates: [[:date<2026-07-01:]] is a date before 1
July, [[:date:2026-01-01..2026-06-30:]] one in the first half of the
year, as RFC 3339 writes them (2026-06-30T14:05:00+02:00).
[[:dateutc<2026-07-01:]] compares the moments in UTC, offsets taken off;
[[:datedmy:]] reads 30.06.2026 and [[:datemdy:]] 06/30/2026.


Lesson 16 -- cursors in a column
--------------------------------

\[add-cursor-below] puts a second cursor on the line below, at the same column, and
every press adds one more below the last.  Then every cursor does what
the cursor does: the motions, the selections, typing, deleting,
copying and pasting all happen at each of them.

>> Put the cursor at the start of the milk line and press \[add-cursor-below] twice.
   Press \[tuatara-insert-line-start], type - and \`SPC', and press \`C-g': the three lines are a list,
   and the cursors are still there.  \`C-g' once more ends them.

   ----> milk
   ----> eggs
   ----> bread

With lines selected, \[add-cursor-below] gives every one of them a cursor at once.
\[add-cursor-above] adds a cursor above instead of below, and \`DEL' takes back
the one added last.

>> Put the cursor on the mercury line, press \`V' \`j' \`j' to select
   three lines, and \[add-cursor-below].  Every line starts with a cursor.  Press
   \`i', type * and \`SPC', and \`C-g' twice.

   ----> mercury
   ----> venus
   ----> earth

>> Put the cursor at the start of the neptune line and press
   \[add-cursor-above] three times: the cursors climb to jupiter.  One is too many, so
   press \`DEL' and the jupiter cursor goes.  Press \`i', type > and \`SPC',
   and \`C-g' twice.  Jupiter is left as it was.

   ----> jupiter
   ----> saturn
   ----> uranus
   ----> neptune

Words differ in length, so after a motion the cursors can stand in
different columns.  \`=' puts every cursor back under the real one.

>> Put the cursor at the start of the one line and press
   \[add-cursor-below] twice, then \[forward-word]: each cursor stops at the end of
   its own first word.  Press \`=' and they line up under the real
   one.  Press \`i', type |, and \`C-g' twice.

   ----> one two three
   ----> x yy zzz
   ----> alpha beta

\`C-g' in INSERT state brings you back to the cursors rather than ending
them, so one set of cursors can do several things in turn.

>> Put the cursor on ada and press \[add-cursor-below] twice.  \[select-word] selects the word at
   every cursor and \[copy-selection-or-char] copies all three.  Press \[tuatara-insert-line-end], type \`SPC' = \`SPC' and
   \`C-g', then \[yank-over-selection]: each line gets its own name back, for ada = ada.  \`C-g'.

   ----> ada
   ----> alan
   ----> grace

\[mark-run-toggle] at the cursors is the mark run from Lesson 5 at every cursor:
each cursor selects its word, and the run's keys grow every selection
at once.

>> Put the cursor at the start of the first fruit line and press
   \[add-cursor-below] twice.  Press \[mark-run-toggle] and \`w': every cursor holds two
   words.  Press \`c', type fresh, and \`C-g' twice.

   ----> old red apple
   ----> old green pear
   ----> old blue plum

A delimiter wraps what each cursor has selected, and the same key
takes the pair off again, as it does with one cursor; with nothing
selected it does nothing.

>> Put the cursor on the first flower below and press \[add-cursor-below] twice,
   then \[select-word] and \`"': every flower is quoted.  Press \`"' again
   and the quotes come off.  \`C-g' twice.

   ----> rose
   ----> iris
   ----> lily

\[delete-selection-or-char] and \[kill-line] delete at every cursor and keep the
cursors, \`V' selects every cursor's line, and \[bank-selected-lines] and
\[unbank-line] bank and unbank those lines.

The case keys work at the cursors too: \`M-u' upcases each selection,
or the word at each cursor, and \`M-l' and \`M-c' lower it and
capitalise it.  \[tuatara-open-below] and \[tuatara-open-above] open a line at every cursor, \[indent-selection-or-line] and \[toggle-comment-lines] indent
and comment every cursor's line, \[repeat] does the last thing again at every
cursor, and \[undo] takes back an edit at all of them at once and keeps the
cursors.

What the cursors copy goes on the kill ring one line per cursor, and a
paste with as many lines as there are cursors gives each its own.

\[split-selection] from the cursors searches only their lines, or only their selections
when they have them.

>> Put the cursor on the first number line and press \[add-cursor-below] twice.  Press
   \[split-selection], type , and \`RET', then \`c' and type ;.  The commas change on the
   three lines and not on the fourth.  \`C-g'.

   ----> 1,2,3
   ----> 4,5,6
   ----> 7,8,9
   ----> 0,0,0

A cursor never leaves its own line: a motion that would cross stops at
the line's edge.  Cursors that come to share a line become one, and a
command that fails at one cursor is undone at all of them.  A key the
cursors do not answer -- \`j' and \`k' among them -- ends them and does its
ordinary job.


The other keys still work
-------------------------

Modal editing is meant to be an addition, not a replacement.  In BOTH states
the \`C-x' prefix, \[execute-extended-command], \`C-h', \`C-s', the arrow keys and the Meta
keys behave as they do without modal editing.  Nothing was taken away to make
room for the letters above.

A modal editor cannot be entirely free, though, and the price is short
enough to state in full.

In INSERT state, one key changes: \`C-g' returns to NORMAL state.  It still
clears the selection, and in the minibuffer it still quits, so the escape
hatch is where you expect it.

In NORMAL state, four things differ:

  - Letters run commands instead of typing.  That is the whole idea.
  - Digits are not counts.  \`3 j' says "3 is undefined" and moves one
    line; \`C-u 3' \[next-line] moves down three.
  - \`RET' does nothing in a buffer you are editing -- a stray newline in
    NORMAL state is rarely what was meant.
  - \`DEL' and \`<delete>' do nothing, so a slip cannot damage the
    buffer from NORMAL state.  Use \[delete-selection-or-char].

>> Try it: press \`C-x' \`C-s' below, or \[execute-extended-command] and then \`C-g' to abandon it.
   Neither is a modal key, and both work from NORMAL state as usual.

Searching: \`C-s' forward and \`C-r' back, in every state; the prompt says
which match you are at and how many there are.  Replacing is on
\[replace-regexp] (all at once) and \[query-replace-regexp] (asking at each),
typed as pattern/replacement: the first match on each line of what is
selected, or of the line you are on, and every match with % in front.
\`M-r' while typing switches between a pattern and plain text.  The
word under the cursor is offered as the pattern.  \[split-selection] edits
every match at once.  \`M-p' brings back what you typed before.
\[describe-bindings] lists the bindings.

Every buffer a program made for you -- help, *Messages*, the buffer
list -- is a SUPPORT buffer: \`h' \`j' \`k' \`l' and the \`SPC' leader are
the modal keys, and every other key is the buffer's own.  The mode line says
TUATARA[S].


That is the working set
-----------------------

\[describe-bindings] lists every binding whenever you want the full picture.
Everything above takes a count, and counts always mean the same thing.

Kill this buffer when you are done.
