* The daemon and other machines

One editor that keeps running, and the screens that show it.

Until now each time you started =tuatara= you got an editor of its own. Its
buffers lived as long as it did. When you closed the terminal they were gone,
unless the session setting of chapter 7 brought them back. This chapter shows
another way. You start one editor with no screen of its own, and then look at
it through as many screens as you like. That editor is called a daemon. It
keeps running when you close a screen, so your buffers, your undo history and
your searches are still there when you come back.

The same idea reaches further. The daemon can run on another computer, a
server, and you can look at it from your own desk. The files, the programs and
the work all stay on the server. Your computer draws the screen and sends your
keys. This is not the same as opening a single file over ssh, which chapter 9
covers. There the editor runs here and only the file is far away. Here the
whole editor is far away.

You will need a terminal for the commands at the start. Everything inside the
editor is pressed in NORMAL state unless it starts with ~C-x~ or ~C-c~. ~i~
starts INSERT state, and ~C-g~ or ~ESC~ returns to NORMAL state.

** One editor in the background

A daemon is useful when you work on the same things all day and open and close
terminals as you go. You start it once, in the morning. Each terminal you open
after that shows it. Close the terminal at lunch and the editor does not
notice. Open another in the afternoon and everything is as you left it.

*** The commands you type in a terminal

These are not editor commands. You type them in your shell, the program that
reads what you type in a terminal.

- =tuatara --daemon= starts the editor in the background and gives you your
  prompt back once it is ready. It reads your init file and loads your
  packages first, which can take a moment, and then says that the daemon runs.
- =tuatara --client= shows the daemon in this terminal. With file names after
  it, those files are opened in the daemon. A client reads no init file of its
  own, because the daemon has already read yours.
- =tuatara --client --start= does the same, but starts the daemon first if
  none is running. Many people use only this one.
- =tuatara --client --gui= shows the daemon in a window of its own instead of
  in the terminal.
- =tuatara --stop= stops the daemon. It asks about any change you have not
  saved, as described under "Stopping the daemon" below.

Words that mean the same as for plain =tuatara= work after =--client= too: a
line number such as =+40= before a file name, =--safe= and =--hex=. Chapter 12
explains the safe visit.

A /client/ is one screen showing the daemon, with a frame of its own and
buffers of its own. When you close a client, its buffers stay in the daemon.
Another client can take them up. A client started while the daemon is still
starting waits for it and says so.

*** Names for several daemons

You can run more than one daemon. Give each a name, made of letters, digits,
=-= and =_=, at most forty characters long. A daemon with no name is called
=default=.

#+begin_example
tuatara --daemon=work
tuatara --client=work
tuatara --stop=work
#+end_example

One daemon for your work and another for your home projects keeps their
buffers apart. =--start= goes with a name as well: =tuatara --client=work
--start=.

*** Closing a client

~C-x C-c~ in a client closes that client. Its screen goes, your terminal is
yours again, and the daemon carries on. Buffers with changes you have not
saved stay as they are, ready for the next client. To end the daemon itself,
use =daemon-quit= or =tuatara --stop=.

A client also closes when its last buffer is closed. If you press ~C-x k~ on
the only buffer a client has, the client ends as if you had pressed ~C-x C-c~.
The scratch buffer that every new client starts with does not count while you
have typed nothing in it.

Two clients can have the same buffer, when both opened the same file. ~C-x k~
in one of them only takes the buffer from that client: its windows show
another of its own buffers, and the other client keeps the buffer with any
changes not saved, without a question. When the last client that has it kills
it, the buffer goes, asking first about unsaved changes. A client that has
ended does not count. ~D~ in the buffer list and ~C-x p k~ leave a shared
buffer in the same way, while ~C-u C-x p k~ kills the project's buffers of
every client. Letting go of a client's last buffer closes the client, as
killing it does.

*** More than one window of a client

~C-x 5 2~ opens another frame of the same client, with the same buffers. In a
terminal it takes turns with the first in that terminal. In a window of the
desktop it is a second window. ~C-x 5 0~ closes the frame you are in. The
client ends when its last frame closes.

*** The D in the mode line

While your editor runs as a daemon, its mode line, and that of each client,
shows a =D= after the other signs. The sign =P= before it is for work in the
background that chapter 11 describes. The =D= is lit while there is a daemon
and faint otherwise, and it never moves, so nothing after it jumps about. The
setting =*daemon-indicator*= is the letter, and =nil= takes it away. The face
=:mode-line-sign= is what it is drawn in.

** Clients in the buffer list

Each client has its own buffers. ~C-x b~ offers yours, and the buffers that
belong to nobody, such as =*Messages*=. ~C-x C-b~ shows the list of chapter 7
with a section for each client in place of the sections for projects, so you
can see what each screen has open.

A buffer you open or make in a client is that client's. The buffers the daemon
had before its first client came, such as its =*lisp-scratch*= and
=*org-scratch*=, are every client's and stay so. You can show them in any
client, and they stay in the group =Every client's=. Each client starts on a
scratch buffer of its own instead, numbered when the name is taken
(=*lisp-scratch*<2>=), and ~M-x org-scratch~ in a client shows its own
=*org-scratch*=, made for it the first time.

Figure 18.1 shows the list in a client that was started at two o'clock, with
one client that has already ended. The client has files of two projects open,
so its directory holds a section for each.

#+caption: Figure 18.1. The buffer list of a daemon with two clients.
#+name: fig-client-list
#+begin_example
   MR  Name                    Size Mode             Filename/Process
   --  ----                    ---- ----             ----------------
▾ This client: client 2 since 14:02, ~/notes (3) (persistent)
  ▾ notes (2)
    >*   todo.org               1,044 Org              ~/notes/todo.org
         ideas.org                310 Org              ~/notes/ideas.org

  ▾ letters (1)
         bank.txt                 880 Text             ~/letters/bank.txt

#+end_example

Your own client comes first, marked =This client=, with the directory it was
started in. Other running clients follow, folded. The clients that have ended
come after them in a faint colour, the last to end first. Their buffers are
still in the daemon, and the list keeps them until you take them up or kill
them. The last section holds the buffers every client is offered, in one
section whatever projects they are in.

Within a client's group, the buffers are in a section for each project, in
the order of the list of chapter 7, each heading under the text of the client's
and its buffers under the text of its own, as chapter 7 has them. A client whose
buffers are all in one project, or all in none, shows them straight under its
heading. ~TAB~ folds a project's section on its own. Folding the client folds
its projects with it, and they come back as they were when you unfold it. ~D~ on
a project's heading kills that client's buffers of the project, and the other
clients keep theirs; ~D~ on the client's heading kills its whole group. ~C-x
p k~ kills your client's buffers of the project you are in, and ~C-u C-x p k~
those of every client, as chapter 7 says.

On the heading of a client that has ended, ~RET~ takes its buffers up into your
client. ~d~ there marks every buffer of that group for deletion, and ~x~
kills them, asking first about unsaved changes. Starting =tuatara --client= with
no file names does the same as ~RET~ for the client that ended last. The face
=:list-dim= is the faint colour of the ended clients.

*** The client that stays

When the last buffer of a client is closed, the client closes. That would
leave a daemon with no screen to show you. So one client may stay open, left
on a scratch buffer of its own, made for it when it has none. It never takes
a buffer of another client or of the group =Every client's=. The setting
=*client-kept-without-buffers*= says which:

| Value   | The client that stays                                |
|---------+------------------------------------------------------|
| =:first= | the one opened first of those still running (standard) |
| =:last=  | only the last one left                              |
| =:all=   | every client                                         |
| =nil=    | none; every client closes with its last buffer       |

The heading of the client that stays says =(persistent)=, as in Figure 18.1.
To choose another, put the cursor in that client's section of the buffer list
and press ~K~. That client stays until it ends. With =:all= there is nothing to
choose.

The buffers of encrypted files are an exception. They close with their client,
and what was copied from them goes too, so that their text does not outlive
the person who opened them.

** Stopping the daemon

~M-x daemon-quit~ in any client, or =tuatara --stop= in a terminal, stops the
daemon. It asks about every change you have not saved, in every buffer of
every client, those of ended clients included, one group at a time. For each
group you press one of three keys:

- ~s~ saves the changes of that group.
- ~l~ leaves them unsaved. They are lost.
- ~C-g~ stops the question and keeps the daemon running.

The daemon ends only when the saves you chose have been written. If you press
~C-g~ while it waits, it carries on running and says that saves are still
under way. Every other client is told that the daemon ends, gives its terminal
back and exits.

=tuatara --stop= typed where there is no terminal, in a script, cannot ask. It
stops the daemon only when no change would be lost. It writes unsaved changes
as auto-saved copies, if the auto-save setting of chapter 7 allows it. When
some change has no copy, the daemon goes on, and the message says how to stop
it from a terminal. When the system itself asks the daemon to end, as at
logout, there is also no one to ask. It writes its auto-saved copies first and
waits up to thirty seconds for saves already begun.

A daemon told to stop always ends. If it has not ended in 60 seconds, or in 90
when there was no terminal to ask, the daemon is ended all the same, so that a
new daemon of that name can start. Before it goes, it writes in its log
(=~/.cache/tuatara/daemon-NAME.log=) which threads were running and the calls
its editor was waiting in. The log names functions only, never what they were
given. Read it when a daemon seems to have stopped late.

** Sessions for each client

The session setting =*keep-session*= of chapter 7 works for a daemon too. The
daemon keeps one session file for each daemon name, written client by client, so
the windows of one client are never mixed with another's. If the daemon is
started again, each client's group is there as one that ended, and =tuatara
--client= with no file names takes up the one that ended last. Files opened
safely are not opened again by themselves. They wait, closed, in their
group, and open safely when a client takes the group up.

#+begin_try Try it, with the daemon stopped and started again
1. Add =(setf *keep-session* t)= to your init file and restart the daemon
   with =tuatara --stop= followed by =tuatara --daemon=.
2. Type =tuatara --client ~/notes.txt=, then press ~C-x C-c~. Type =tuatara
   --stop= and answer ~s~ if asked.
3. Start the daemon again and type =tuatara --client=. The group of the
   client that ended last is taken up, and =notes.txt= is open.
#+end_try

** A daemon on another machine

Suppose you have a small server called =pinetree=, in a cupboard in Taupo,
and you keep your notes and a few projects on it. With a daemon there you
open your editor from any computer and find it as you left it, with the files
next to the programs that build them. Language servers, git and terminal
buffers all run on pinetree. Your computer draws the screen.

#+caption: Figure 18.2. A client here, a daemon there, ssh between them.
#+name: fig-client-daemon-ssh
#+begin_example
 your computer                              pinetree
 +-------------------+                      +---------------------+
 | tuatara --client= |    ssh (OpenSSH)     | tuatara daemon      |
 | draws the screen  |<====================>| files, git, builds, |
 | sends your keys   |    keys, screen      | language servers    |
 +-------------------+                      +---------------------+
   clipboard, agents and dropped files cross only for a trusted server
#+end_example

*** Starting, using and stopping

Name the server before the daemon's name, with a colon between them:

#+begin_example
tuatara --client=pinetree:
tuatara --client=aroha@pinetree.example:work
tuatara --client=pinetree: --start todo.org
tuatara --stop=pinetree:
#+end_example

The first shows the default daemon on pinetree. The second shows the daemon
called =work= there, as the user =aroha=. The third starts a daemon on
pinetree first if none runs, and opens =todo.org= there. The last stops it.

A name with a dot or an =@= in it is taken as a server. A plain name such as
=--client=work= is a daemon on this computer. A server named only by an alias
from your =~/.ssh/config= file needs a colon after it, as in =pinetree:=.

The files you name are the server's. A relative name such as =notes.org= is
=notes.org= in your home directory on pinetree, and =/etc/hosts= is the file of
that name there.

*** What has to be in place

The connection is made by the ssh program of your system, as if you had typed
=ssh pinetree=, with your keys and your =~/.ssh/config=. If ssh needs a
passphrase it asks in your terminal first. You need to have connected to the
server once before, and checked its host key, because a host whose key is not
known is refused. You also need =setpriv=, a small program from the util-linux
package, which ties the connection to the client so that it ends when the
client does, even if the client is killed.

On the server you need the same build of Tuatara. If it is older, or not
there, the client tells you and exits. If Tuatara is not on the server's
=PATH=, name it in =M-x server-list= with ~T~, as shown below.

The server has to run OpenSSH. A daemon takes a client from another machine
only when OpenSSH's own server carries the connection. It refuses a
connection through any other ssh server. The daemon must run under an ordinary
account, directly on the server, not as root and not in a container.

If the server removes your runtime directory when you log out, the daemon's
socket goes with it. =--start= says so. On a server run by systemd, the
command =loginctl enable-linger= keeps the directory.

*** Keys limited with command=

An entry in the server's =authorized_keys= file may be limited with a
=command== setting. That does not stop the key from forwarding a connection to
the daemon's socket. If you give such a key to someone, add =restrict= or
=no-port-forwarding= to the same entry.

*** What a server is given

A server you have not trusted gets the keys you type, the size of your screen
and the names of the files you open. Nothing else of your computer reaches it.
Pictures are drawn on the server and sent to your window as small pictures of
the size shown, so a page of a large scan does not cross the network whole.
Whatever your =~/.ssh/config= says, ssh is told not to forward X11, Kerberos
tickets, or an agent, and not to run local or remote commands.

A client closes with ~C-x C-c~ and leaves the daemon on pinetree running. A
client that is killed, or whose terminal is closed, takes its ssh connection
with it.

** The trusted servers

Some things are useful across the connection: the clipboard, your keys for
signing, files dropped on the window. Each is off until you trust a server
with it. ~M-x server-list~ shows the trusted servers in a buffer called
=*Servers*=, a line for each. A server not on the list is given nothing.

~M-x server-trust~ adds a machine, written as =--client= takes it. Note that
=pinetree.example= and =aroha@pinetree.example= are two entries. A new entry
has nothing turned on. Each thing is then turned on by a key on its line,
after Tuatara asks and says what the server will be given. Turning a thing
off asks nothing.

*** The columns

#+begin_example
 M Server            Agent Keys used for  GPG Clipboard Dropped files to Trusted
   aroha@pinetree    yes   github.com     no  yes       Dropped          2026-11-04
   napier.example    no                   no  no        no               2026-11-06
#+end_example

| Column           | What it says                                                                        |
|------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| M                | A mark (=>= or =D=) put on the line.                                                |
| Server           | The machine, as =--client= names it.                                                |
| Agent            | Whether your ssh agent is forwarded there.                                          |
| Keys used for    | The machines the agent's keys may be used for from there; =any= when none is named. |
| GPG              | Whether gpg-agent is forwarded there.                                               |
| Clipboard        | Whether the clipboard is shared with it.                                            |
| Dropped files to | The directory there that dropped files are sent to, or =no=.                        |
| Trusted          | The day you trusted the machine.                                                    |
| Tuatara there    | The program to run there, if not the one on its =PATH=.                             |

*** The keys of the list

Each key acts on the servers marked with ~m~, or on the line the cursor is on
when none is marked.

| Modal | Non-modal | What it does                                                  |
|-------+-----------+---------------------------------------------------------------|
| none  | ~+~       | trust another machine, with nothing turned on                 |
| none  | ~A~       | forward the ssh agent, or stop                                |
| none  | ~H~       | name the machines the agent's keys may be used for from there |
| none  | ~G~       | forward gpg-agent, or stop                                    |
| none  | ~C~       | share the clipboard, or stop                                  |
| none  | ~F~       | send dropped files to a directory there, or stop              |
| none  | ~T~       | name the tuatara to run there                                 |
| none  | ~D~       | forget the server, after asking                               |

The list is kept in a file named =ssh-servers= in Tuatara's state directory. A
line it cannot read gives its server nothing.

*** The ssh agent

An ssh agent holds your private keys and signs for you, so a program that
needs to log in somewhere does not have to ask for your passphrase. With ~A~ on
for a server, the programs there, git for example, can sign with your keys
while a client shows that daemon. Tuatara does not hand over the agent you
keep your keys in. The client starts an agent of its own for this connection
and puts in it only the keys your ssh configuration names for that server. Each
key is added so that every use of it brings up a question on your screen, and
nothing is signed until you say yes. If there is no program to ask the
question, the client says so and does not connect.

~H~ limits where the keys may be used from the server. With =github.com=
named, the server can use them for github.com and nothing else. Write the
names as they appear in your =known_hosts= file. This needs OpenSSH 8.9 or
later on both machines. If the limit cannot be applied, the client refuses to
connect. To forward the agent with no limit, name no machine.

The agent reaches only the programs of that client's frames. Another client
of the same daemon sees none of it, and it ends with the connection.

*** The gpg agent

GnuPG is a program for signing and encrypting, and gpg-agent holds its keys.
With ~G~ on, gpg on the server signs and decrypts with your keys, which stay on
your computer. The passphrase is asked here. The forwarded socket cannot export
or change your keys, so nothing on the server can take them. While gpg-agent
here keeps the passphrase, the server can use that key without asking again.

Two things need setting on the server. gpg there must not start an agent of
its own, so put the line =no-autostart= in =gpg.conf= in GnuPG's directory there
(=gpgconf --list-dirs homedir= says where it is, usually =~/.gnupg=). And the
ssh server should remove a leftover socket before forwarding a new one, which
means adding =StreamLocalBindUnlink yes= to =/etc/ssh/sshd_config= as root.
When the client cannot tell whether that is set, it says what to add.

To encrypt a file on the server to someone else, gpg there needs their public
key. In the list of your keys (=M-x gpg-list-keys=), mark the keys with ~m~ and
run =M-x server-copy-keys=. It asks for the trusted server and sends the public
keys over ssh in the background. Nothing secret is sent.

*** The clipboard and dropped files

With ~C~ on, the clipboard goes both ways while the client's window has the
keyboard. What you copy in the daemon reaches the clipboard on your computer,
and the daemon may read what you copied here. Text that comes from the server
loses its control characters, so that pasting it into a terminal cannot carry
a hidden escape sequence. Without ~C~, nothing of the clipboard crosses.

With ~F~ on, a file you drop onto the window showing the server's daemon is sent
into a directory there, =Dropped= in your home directory unless you give another
name. A name beginning with =/= is taken from the root. The file is written for
you alone under a name nothing else has, and nothing in it is run. Only files
are sent, at most 100 MB each.

*** A language server for a server

A language server runs where the daemon runs. If pinetree has none installed
and you cannot install one, =M-x server-bring-language-server= copies one from
your computer, in the background, to a directory of the editor's own on the
server. Nothing is fetched from the internet. It must be a program for the
server's kind of machine that needs nothing but the usual system libraries.
=M-x server-remove-language-server= takes it away again.
On your own computer such servers are kept in =tuatara/language-servers/= under
=$XDG_DATA_HOME= (=~/.local/share= when that is not set), and are looked for
after the directories of your =PATH=.
