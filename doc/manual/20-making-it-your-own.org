* Making it your own

Changing Tuatara to suit you, and keeping the changes.

Every chapter so far has said "as standard" about something: the font, the
colours, the keys, the way a journal entry is named. This chapter is about
changing those. You do it with a small file of your own, called the init
file, which Tuatara reads each time it starts. Nothing you put in it is ever
overwritten by an update.

The chapter shows what the file is and where Tuatara keeps it, how to change
a setting, how to give a key to a command, how to move or turn off the modal
states, how to pick a theme and a font, and how to write snippets of your
own. Two things that nothing else in the manual covers, the calendar and the
journal, have a short section each. The last section holds the odds and ends
that have no better home.

You do not need to know Lisp. Every change in this chapter is one line that
you copy, with a word or two changed.

** The init file

The init file is a plain text file called =init.lisp= in a folder of its own,
=~/.config/tuatara/=. The first time Tuatara runs and finds no file there, it
writes one for you. The message =Wrote a starter init file= says so. After
that Tuatara never writes it again. It is yours.

The starter file lists every setting there is. Each one sits in a comment (a
line that starts with =;;= is a comment, and Tuatara skips it), with a few
words saying what the setting does and, after =Not set:=, what it is when you
leave it alone. To use a setting you take away the =;;= and the space after
it. To go back to the standard, you put them back.

#+begin_example
  ;; Two keys typed quickly one after the other in INSERT state leave it for
  ;; NORMAL state, and neither stays in the text. Not set: nil.
  ;; (setf *insert-escape-keys* "jk")
#+end_example

The block above is as the starter file has it. With the last line's =;;=
taken away, it reads =(setf *insert-escape-keys* "jk")= and the setting is on.
Everything is in groups, each begun by a banner of equals signs. Search for
three of them with ~C-s~ to jump from one group to the next.

Tuatara reads the file once, from the top, each time it starts. A change you
make is therefore there the next time you start. You do not have to start
again to try it. Put the cursor just after the closing bracket of the line
and press ~C-x C-e~. That runs the line in the editor you are using, and the
echo area shows what it returned. The line stays in the file, so the change
is still there tomorrow.

If a line in the file is wrong, say a bracket is missing or a name is
misspelt, Tuatara tells you on the echo row, with the line, and goes on to
read the rest. It always starts. You can open the file and mend it.

To open the file, press ~C-x C-f~, type =~/.config/tuatara/init.lisp= and
press ~RET~.

#+begin_try Try it, with the init file
1. Press ~C-x C-f~, type =~/.config/tuatara/init.lisp= and press ~RET~.
2. Press ~g g~ (non-modal: ~M-<~), then ~C-s~, type =fill-column= and press
   ~RET~. The search stops on the line =;; (setf *fill-column* 72)=, which has a
   comment above it about the column that ~M-q~ (modal: ~g Q~) fills to.
3. Press ~g h~ (non-modal: ~C-a~) to go to the start of the line, then ~l~ until
   you are on the first =;=. Press ~d~ three times to take away both semicolons
   and the space.
4. Press ~g l~ (non-modal: ~C-e~) to go to the end of the line, then ~C-x C-e~.
   The echo area shows =72=.
5. Press ~C-h v~, type =*fill-column*= and press ~RET~. The help says the
   value is 72.
6. Press ~C-x C-s~ to save. Starting Tuatara again keeps the 72.
#+end_try

** Where Tuatara keeps its files

Tuatara keeps what it makes in three places, each with a different job. The
division follows the standard that most programs on Linux follow, so a tool
that backs up or cleans your home folder already knows about them.

- The configuration folder, =~/.config/tuatara/=, holds what you wrote or
  chose. This is the init file, your snippets, your note and export
  templates, and your logos. If you lose it you lose your choices.
- The state folder, =~/.local/state/tuatara/=, holds what Tuatara
  remembers about how you work: the files you opened lately, where you were
  in each, your bookmarks, the session, and the copies of unsaved changes.
  If you lose it nothing you wrote is gone, but Tuatara forgets its habits.
- The cache folder, =~/.cache/tuatara/=, holds things Tuatara can make
  again: previews, pages drawn from documents, copies of files opened
  safely, compiled packages and the log of what happened. You can delete it
  at any time.

Fetched packages are kept in a fourth place, =~/.local/share/tuatara/=,
described in chapter 19.

Figure 20.1 shows the folders and what each holds.

#+caption: Figure 20.1. The folders Tuatara uses, and what is in each.
#+name: fig-where-files-live
#+begin_example
 ~/.config/tuatara/            what you wrote
   init.lisp                     your settings and keys
   snippets/                     your snippets, a folder for each mode
   note-templates/ ...           templates and logos for notes and exports
   logos/

 ~/.local/state/tuatara/       what Tuatara remembers
   recent-files   places   bookmarks   session
   auto-save/                    copies of changes not yet saved

 ~/.cache/tuatara/             what can be made again
   debug.log   stderr.log        what Tuatara said
   previews, pages, compiled packages ...

 ~/.local/share/tuatara/       what was fetched
   packages/                     packages from other people
#+end_example

If your computer sets =XDG_CONFIG_HOME=, =XDG_STATE_HOME=, =XDG_CACHE_HOME= or
=XDG_DATA_HOME=, Tuatara uses a folder called =tuatara= inside each of them
instead of the ones in the figure. You can also set the folders yourself,
which is useful when you keep them on an encrypted drive. The three settings
below do it. Make the folder first, with =mkdir -m 700=, so that only you can
read it. While the folder is not there, because the drive is not mounted,
Tuatara keeps those things in memory and says so.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *state-directory* "/run/media/aroha/Data/tuatara/state/")
#+end_src

=*cache-directory*= is the same for the cache folder. It is =nil=, which
means =~/.cache/tuatara/= (or =tuatara= inside =XDG_CACHE_HOME=), or the name
of a folder. Files you open from outside leave copies in the cache, so a
cache on an encrypted drive keeps those copies off the plain disk:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *cache-directory* "/run/media/aroha/Data/tuatara/cache/")
#+end_src

*** What to copy to another computer

To have your Tuatara on another computer, copy the configuration folder,
=~/.config/tuatara/=, and nothing else. It holds the init file and everything
you wrote. Packages are fetched again on the new computer when the init file
asks for them.

Copy the state folder as well if you want the new computer to know your
recent files and bookmarks. Do not copy the cache. It is rebuilt as you
work, and parts of it are only right for the computer that made them.

Paths in the init file, such as a folder for your journal, must exist on
the new computer too.

** Changing a setting

A setting is a name that holds a value. Its name starts and ends with a star,
=*fill-column*=, so that it is easy to tell from a command. You change one
with =setf=, which means "set to":

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *fill-column* 72)
#+end_src

The line reads: set =*fill-column*= to 72. The value can be a number, a word
in quotes, =t= for yes, =nil= for no, or a name that starts with a colon, such
as =:bar=. The help of each setting says which it takes.

~C-h v~ asks for a setting's name and shows its value, what it means, and
where it was defined. It is the quickest way to find out what you can put
after the name. ~M-:~ lets you type a line without opening the init file,
which is useful to try a value out before you save it.

Some settings hold a list of things, for example the modes that do something.
To add to one instead of replacing it, put your item in front of what it
holds:

#+begin_src lisp
(push "C-;" *terminal-editor-keys*)
#+end_src

A few settings are better said with a function than with =setf=, because
they take a mode and a value. The starter file has these written out, for
example =(set-tab-width 'c-mode 8)=. Keep to the form the starter file shows.

Chapter 21 lists every setting and what it is as standard.

** Keys of your own

~bind-key~ gives a key to a command. It is the main command of the init file.
You give it the keys and the command's name, which is the name that ~C-h f~
and ~M-x~ show. Keys are written as the help writes them: =C-= is Control,
=M-= is Meta (Alt on most keyboards) and =S-= is Shift. Keys that you press one
after the other are separated by a space.

#+begin_src lisp
(bind-key "C-c t" 'toggle-light-dark-theme)
#+end_src

After that line, ~C-c t~ switches between the light and the dark theme in
every buffer and in both states. The mark before the command's name is a
single quote, and it belongs there.

Where a key goes depends on the map you name with =:map=. Table 20.1 lists the
forms you will use. In all of them you can put any command in place of the one
shown.

#+caption: Table 20.1. The ways to bind a key.
| Form                                            | What it does                      |
|-------------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------|
| =(bind-key "C-c t" 'command)=                   | The key works in every buffer and both states |
| =(bind-key "o" 'command :map :leader)=          | ~SPC o~ in NORMAL state, and ~C-x SPC o~ everywhere |
| =(bind-key "e" 'command :map :normal)=          | ~e~ in NORMAL state only |
| =(bind-key "C-c e" 'command :map 'csv-mode)=    | The key in CSV buffers only |
| =(bind-key "m l" 'command :map :leader :mode '(org-mode markdown-mode))= | ~SPC m l~ in Org and Markdown only |
| =(bind-key "t n" '("numbers" . command) :map :leader)= | ~SPC t n~, shown as "numbers" in the key hints |
| =(bind-key "t" nil :map :leader :label "toggles")= | The label of a prefix: ~SPC~ then shows =t +toggles= |
| =(unbind-key "C-c t")=                          | Takes a key away |

Three more things about the table. First, the maps. =:global= is the standard,
every buffer in both states. =:normal=, =:insert= and =:support= are the keys of
the NORMAL state, the INSERT state and the buffers that Tuatara makes itself,
such as lists and help. =:leader= is the leader, described in the next
section. Other maps are named after one kind of question: =:pattern=, for
instance, is every question that asks for a pattern to search for. A map can
also be the name of a major mode, as the CSV line shows. Then the key is
only there in buffers of that mode and the modes made from it.

Second, =:mode= is a quicker way to say the same. It takes one mode or a
list. A key bound for a mode never changes what the same key does in other
modes, so you can give ~SPC m l~ one meaning in Org and another in code
without either getting in the other's way.

Third, labels. The key hints (chapter 8) show the name of a command beside
each key. A label is a name of your own to show instead. The two ways to give
one are in the table: put the label and the command together in a pair, or
bind a prefix to =nil= and give it a label.

Which keys are free? ~C-h k~ followed by a key says what the key does now. A key
with nothing bound says so. ~C-c~ followed by a letter is a good place to start,
and ~C-c t~ is free as standard. The letters ~h j k l~ only ever move the cursor
in NORMAL state. Do not give them another job.

#+begin_try Try it, with a key of your own
1. Open the init file with ~C-x C-f~ and go to its end with ~g e~ (non-modal:
   ~M->~).
2. Press ~o~ to open a new line and type this line.
   =(bind-key "C-c t" 'toggle-light-dark-theme)=
3. Press ~C-g~ to go back to NORMAL state, then ~C-x C-e~. The echo area
   shows the name of the command.
4. Press ~C-c t~. The colours turn light. Press it again and they are dark
   again.
5. Press ~C-x C-s~ to save. To take the key away again, put =(unbind-key
   "C-c t")= under the line, or put =;;= in front of it.
#+end_try

** A leader key of your own

The leader is a map of keys that belong to you. As Tuatara comes, nothing is
under it, so there is nothing to learn that you did not put there. In NORMAL
state, in lists and in the calendar, ~SPC~ opens it. ~C-x SPC~ opens it in
every buffer and state, INSERT state and a session with modal editing off
too, so a leader key works wherever you are.

The starter file lists, commented out, a set of leader keys that suit many
people: ~SPC f~ to find a file, ~SPC b~ to go to the other buffer, ~SPC w~ for
a family of window keys, and so on. Take away the =;;= in front of the ones you
want, or write your own with =:map :leader=.

#+begin_src lisp
(bind-key "o" 'find-file :map :leader)
(bind-key "t" nil :map :leader :label "toggles")
(bind-key "t l" 'soft-wrap-mode :map :leader)
(bind-key "t h" 'hl-line-mode :map :leader)
#+end_src

The four lines make ~SPC o~ open a file. They also make ~SPC t~ a prefix with
the label "toggles", with ~SPC t l~ to wrap long lines and ~SPC t h~ to
highlight the line the cursor is on. After you press ~SPC~, the key hints
show what is under it, so you do not have to remember.

If ~SPC~ is a key you would like for something else, move the leader.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *leader-key* ",")
#+end_src

Now the leader is on =,= in NORMAL state, and ~SPC~ is free again. ~C-x SPC~
does not move. Chapter 8 lists the leader's settings with the settings for
modal editing.

** Modal editing, and turning it off

Tuatara comes with modal editing on. A file opens in NORMAL state, where
letters are commands. ~i~ starts INSERT state, where letters are typed, and
~C-g~ goes back. Chapter 1 introduced it.

Some people would rather type at once and use the keys with Control and Meta
for commands, as other editors do. If that is you, turn modal editing off:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *modal-editing* nil)
#+end_src

Every buffer then takes the keys of its mode and the global keys. Typing puts
letters in the text. The states are gone from the mode line. Every command
is still there, to run with ~M-x~ or to bind to a key. The leader stays,
under ~C-x SPC~. The commands that selecting, cursors, spelling and replacing
have in NORMAL state are under ~C-z~, on the same letters: ~C-z m f~ selects
the function around the cursor, ~C-z t~ adds a cursor below.

#+begin_try Try it, with modal editing off
1. In the init file, press ~g e~ (non-modal: ~M->~) and ~o~, and type
   =(setf *modal-editing* nil)=. Press ~C-g~, then ~C-x C-e~. The echo area
   shows =NIL=.
2. Open another file with ~C-x C-f~. The mode line no longer shows a state
   and the letters you type go into the text.
3. To go back, change the line to =(setf *modal-editing* t)= and press
   ~C-x C-e~ again. You can also take the line out of the file.
#+end_try

You can keep modal editing and make leaving INSERT state easier. ~C-g~ is
the key that does it as standard, and so does ~ESC~. If you would rather type
two letters, set a pair that hardly ever follows one another in your
writing.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *insert-escape-keys* "jk")
#+end_src

Typing =j= puts a =j= in the text at once, like any key. If =k= comes within a
fifth of a second, the =j= is taken out again and you are in NORMAL state.
Slower, or with another key between, both letters stay. It works while you
type in an ordinary buffer. It does not work in the minibuffer, in a terminal
or with several cursors.

Chapter 8 describes =*escape-quits*=, which makes ~ESC~ do all that ~C-g~ does.

** Themes and faces

A theme is a set of colours for everything Tuatara draws. A face is the look
of one kind of thing: how a comment is drawn, or the line you are on, or the
mode line. A theme gives every face its look.

*** Choosing a theme

~M-x load-theme~ lists the themes, each with a line saying what it is like,
and puts the one you choose in force. There are four.

- =ao-dark= is the standard: a deep navy ground with an amber accent.
- =ao-light= is the same palette worked for a white ground.
- =terminal= uses the sixteen colours of your terminal, so Tuatara follows
  whatever colours the terminal uses.
- =plain= uses no colours at all, only bold, underline, reverse video and
  dim. It is for terminals that cannot show colour.

The theme you choose with ~M-x load-theme~ lasts until you leave. To keep it,
put it in the init file:

#+begin_src lisp
(load-theme 'ao-light)
#+end_src

In a window of its own, Tuatara follows the desktop. If the desktop turns
dark in the evening, Tuatara switches to its dark theme, and back again in
the morning. =*follow-desktop-style*= set to =nil= stops that and keeps one
theme. ~M-x toggle-light-dark-theme~ switches between the dark and the light
theme by hand. =*light-theme*= and =*dark-theme*= say which two themes it,
and the desktop, switch between.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *light-theme* 'plain)
(setf *dark-theme* 'terminal)
#+end_src

#+begin_try Try it, with a theme
1. Press ~M-x~, type =load-theme= and press ~RET~. Choose =ao-light= with
   the arrow keys and ~RET~. The colours turn light.
2. Press ~M-x~ and run =toggle-light-dark-theme=. They turn dark again.
3. To keep the light theme, open the init file and add
   =(load-theme 'ao-light)= on a line of its own. Press ~C-g~, then
   ~C-x C-e~ and ~C-x C-s~.
#+end_try

*** Changing one face

~M-x list-faces~ shows every face, each with a sample drawn as the theme in
force draws it, and a line saying what it colours. ~M-x describe-face~ shows
all that is known of one face, including what the theme and your init file
set for it. Both are covered in chapter 8.

~set-face~ changes a face. It takes the face's name and then pairs of an
attribute and a value:

#+begin_src lisp
(set-face :comment :fg :cyan :italic t)
(set-face :hl-line :bg "#1a2a3a")
#+end_src

The first line draws comments in cyan italics. The second gives the line you are
on a dark blue ground. A colour is a name such as =:red=, a number from 0 to
255, or a six-digit code in quotes. The attributes are =:fg= and =:bg= for the
text and the ground, =:bold=, =:italic=, =:dim= and =:reverse=, which each take
=t= or =nil=, and =:underline=. In a window of its own a face can also have a
=:font= and a =:height=.

What you set stays on top of any theme. Choose another theme and your comment
colour is still yours.

*** A theme of your own

When a theme is nearly right, define one of your own that changes only what
you do not like. Name it, say in a few words what it is, say which theme to
start from, and give the colours that differ:

#+begin_src lisp
(define-theme my-ao
  "Ao on black."
  :faces-from ao-dark
  :palette ((ground "#000000")))
(load-theme 'my-ao)
#+end_src

The colours of a theme go by role: =ground= is the background, =text= the
text, =comment= the comments, =blue= and =green= and so on. The palette says
what each role is. In the example only =ground= changes, from navy to black,
and everything drawn on the ground follows. After =define-theme=, ~M-x
load-theme~ lists =my-ao= with the others.

** Fonts

In a window of its own (=tuatara --gui=), Tuatara draws the text in a font you
choose. In a terminal the font is the terminal's own, and these commands
say so and change nothing.

To make the text larger or smaller for the moment, press ~C-x C-+~. That makes
it a step larger, about a fifth. Each press of ~+~ after it goes on. ~-~ makes
it smaller, and ~0~ returns to the size it started with. Holding Ctrl and
turning the mouse wheel does the same. The size is for this session. When
you want a size for good, change the font in the init file.

~M-x font-list~ shows every font installed on the computer, the ones whose
letters are all the same width first, each with a sample written in it. Move
to a line and press ~RET~ to see the window in that font now. Press ~w~ to
copy the line that chooses it for good. Then paste it into your init file
with ~C-y~.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *gui-font* "Adwaita Mono 14")
#+end_src

A font is a family, an optional style and a size in points. The text window
works best with a font whose letters are all one width, since the text is
laid in columns. ~M-x set-frame-font~ is another way to the same change by
name: type a family and a size after it. ~<f11>~ makes the window fill the
screen, and ~M-<f10>~ makes it as large as the screen allows with its
title bar. Press either again to go back.

#+begin_try Try it, with a bigger font
1. In a window (=tuatara --gui=), press ~C-x C-+~. The text grows by a step.
   Press ~+~ twice more, then ~0~ to go back to the standard size.
2. Press ~M-x~, type =font-list= and press ~RET~. Move to a line of a font
   you like with ~j~ and ~k~ and press ~RET~. The window changes at once.
3. Press ~w~. The echo area says the line is copied.
4. Open the init file, press ~g e~ (non-modal: ~M->~), ~o~, and paste with
   ~C-y~. Change the size in the quotes to 14. Press ~C-g~, ~C-x C-e~, then
   ~C-x C-s~.
#+end_try

** Your name

Some of what Tuatara writes carries your name: the author line of an
exported document, a note template and a snippet. As standard it uses the
full name your account has on the computer, or else the login name. To use
another, set it:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *user-full-name* "Aroha Smith")
#+end_src

Its table is in chapter 13, with the snippets that use it.

** Snippets of your own

A snippet is a piece of text with places to fill in. You type its short key
and press ~TAB~, and the text goes in with the cursor on the first place.
Chapter 13 tells all about the places. This section is about making a snippet
that no one else has, for something you write often.

A snippet is a file. It lives in =~/.config/tuatara/snippets/=, in a folder
named for the mode that it is for. =fundamental-mode= is for every buffer.
=org-mode= is for Org notes. The file starts with three lines: the name, the
key you type, and a line with two dashes. Everything after the dashes is the
text that goes in. In the text, =$1=, =$2= and so on are places that ~TAB~ moves
through, =$0= is where the cursor ends, and =${1:text}= is a place that begins
with text you can type over. =$AUTHOR= is your name, and =$CURRENT_DATE=,
=$CURRENT_MONTH= and =$CURRENT_YEAR= are today's date.

~C-c y~ (=insert-snippet=) chooses one from a list by name. ~M-x copy-snippet~
copies one of Tuatara's own into your folder, so that you can change it. Yours
then takes its place.

#+begin_try Try it, with a snippet for a letter's greeting
1. In a terminal, type these two commands.
   #+begin_src sh
   mkdir -p ~/.config/tuatara/snippets/fundamental-mode
   printf '# name: Greeting\n# key: dear\n# --\nKia ora ${1:Aroha},\n\n$0\n\nKind regards,\n$AUTHOR\n' > ~/.config/tuatara/snippets/fundamental-mode/greeting
   #+end_src
2. In Tuatara, open a new file with ~C-x C-f~ and the name =~/letter.txt=.
   Press ~i~, type =dear= and press ~TAB~. The greeting goes in, with the name
   ready to type over.
3. Type =Hemi=, press ~TAB~, and the cursor moves to the empty line for your
   letter. Your name is at the bottom.
4. Press ~C-g~, then ~u~ until the text is gone. Delete the snippet file if
   you do not want it.
#+end_try

** How lines are shown

Tuatara can show the same text in several ways without changing a character
of it: the line the cursor is on painted across the window, long lines
wrapped onto several rows, a line down the window at the fill column, the
column the cursor is in painted down the window, the text in a column in the
middle of the window, and the characters that draw nothing shown as their
codes. Each is a mode you switch on or off. They all work the same way.

A command with =-mode= at the end switches the thing for the buffer you are
in. That buffer keeps its choice. Most have a key under ~C-x T~, for "toggle".
The same command with =global-= in front switches it for every buffer that has
not chosen for itself, while Tuatara runs. A setting in the init file says
what every buffer has from the start. A list of modes, such as
=*soft-wrap-modes*=, says it for one kind of buffer, all Org notes or all code,
whatever the setting for every buffer says.

A buffer's own choice counts first, then the list of modes, then the setting
for every buffer. Each switch says what it did on the echo row. With a count
before it, ~C-u 1~, a command switches on whatever it was, and ~C-u 0~
switches it off.

The commands and what they show, with the keys they have, are in the table
below. Some of them are shown in other chapters. Here they are together.

#+table: commands hl-line-mode cursor-column-mode soft-wrap-mode visual-line-mode centred-text-mode invisible-characters-mode text-direction-mode global-hl-line-mode global-soft-wrap-mode global-visual-line-mode global-centred-text-mode global-cursor-column-mode global-invisible-characters-mode global-text-direction-mode

Text that is written from right to left, such as Hebrew and Arabic, is
shown in the order it is read. =text-direction-mode= is for the rare text
where you want each line's letters in the order they are stored instead.

The settings say the same in the init file. Each mode has a pair:
=*global-NAME-mode*= for every buffer, and =*NAME-modes*= for the kinds of
buffer that differ. The first is =t= or =nil=. The second is a list of
=(MODE . VALUE)=, where MODE is the name of a major mode, with or without
=-mode=, and VALUE is =t= or =nil=. A mode counts for the modes made from it,
and the nearest one listed decides. For example, to wrap lines in Org notes
and plain text but not in CSV files, whatever the rest do:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *soft-wrap-modes* '((org-mode . t) (text-mode . t) (csv-mode . nil)))
#+end_src

The pairs, each with what it shows:

- =*global-hl-line-mode*= and =*hl-line-modes*= paint the line the cursor is
  on across the window. It is on for every buffer unless you set the first
  to =nil=. To keep it off in CSV files only:
  =(setf *hl-line-modes* '((csv-mode . nil)))=.
- =*global-soft-wrap-mode*= and =*soft-wrap-modes*= wrap long lines onto
  several rows. They are off unless set, except in help and the about
  buffer, which wrap. =(setf *global-soft-wrap-mode* t)= wraps everywhere.
- =*soft-wrap-at-fill-column*= says where a wrapped line breaks. With =t=,
  the default, at the fill column or the window's edge, whichever is nearer.
  With =nil= always at the window's edge, so on a wide window the lines
  are long.
- =*global-visual-line-mode*= and =*visual-line-modes*= draw a line down the
  window after the fill column, the 80th column when it is 80. Nothing
  wraps. Off unless set: =(setf *visual-line-modes* '((prog-mode . t)))=.
- =*global-centred-text-mode*= and =*centred-text-modes*= show the text in a
  column in the middle of the window. Off unless set:
  =(setf *centred-text-modes* '((org-mode . t) (text-mode . t)))=.
- =*centred-text-width*= is the width of that column, 80 by default. With
  =(setf *centred-text-width* 70)= the text takes 70 columns and the margins
  are blank.
- =*global-cursor-column-mode*= and =*cursor-column-modes*= paint the column
  the cursor is in down the window. Off unless set:
  =(setf *cursor-column-modes* '((csv-mode . t)))= helps to read a table.
- =*global-invisible-characters-mode*= and =*invisible-characters-modes*=
  show characters that draw nothing as their codes, such as =<FEFF>= for a
  byte-order mark. Off unless set, but always on in a file opened safely.
  =(setf *global-invisible-characters-mode* t)= shows them everywhere.
- =*global-text-direction-mode*= and =*text-direction-modes*= say how lines
  of right-to-left text are shown. They take more than =t= and =nil=. The
  default, =:auto=, shows the order read, each line starting at the side its
  first letter is written from. =:right-to-left= and =:left-to-right= do the
  same with every line starting at the side named. =nil= shows each line's
  characters in the order stored. A file opened safely is shown in the order
  stored. =(setf *text-direction-modes* '((csv-mode . nil)))= keeps CSV
  files in the order stored.

#+table: settings *global-hl-line-mode* *hl-line-modes* *global-soft-wrap-mode* *soft-wrap-modes* *soft-wrap-at-fill-column* *global-visual-line-mode* *visual-line-modes* *global-centred-text-mode* *centred-text-modes* *centred-text-width* *global-cursor-column-mode* *cursor-column-modes* *global-invisible-characters-mode* *invisible-characters-modes* *global-text-direction-mode* *text-direction-modes*

** The calendar

~C-x n a~ shows the calendar in a window below the one you are in: the month
of today and the month before and after it, with the cursor on today.
~C-x n A~ shows it in the window you are in instead, in place of its buffer.
~q~ closes it and brings your buffer back.

The arrow keys, or ~h~ ~l~ for a day back or on and ~k~ ~j~ for a week back or
on, move through the days. ~H~ and ~L~ show the month before and after, and ~J~
and ~K~ move the cursor a month. ~.~ goes to today, and ~g l~ goes back to where
you were before you moved. ~g d~ asks for a date and goes to it. You can
answer with =2026-12-24=, with =tomorrow=, =fri= or =+3d=, or as any date is
asked for in Tuatara. ~o~ shows the months around one you name, such as =jan 27=
for January 2027.

Days that have something on them are marked. An agenda entry (chapter 14) marks
its day, and a journal entry (the next section) underlines it. ~RET~ on a day
opens what it is marked for. When a day has both, Tuatara asks which, and each
answer is one key. ~n~ opens the journal entry for the day at the cursor, or
makes it. ~m~ marks the days that have agenda entries and ~u~ takes the marks
off. ~p d~ says which day of the year the cursor is on, and ~p c~ says the week
and the ISO date.

Three settings change how the calendar looks. By default it shows three months
and weeks begin on Monday. =*calendar-months*= set to 12 shows a whole year.
=*calendar-week-start-day*= set to 0 begins weeks on Sunday.
=*calendar-months-per-row*= says how many months stand side by side. It is as
many as fit when it is =nil=.

=*calendar-mark-days*= says whether the days that have agenda entries are
marked as soon as the calendar shows them. It is =t= by default. With =nil=
the calendar opens without marks and ~m~ puts them on when you want them,
which is quicker if you have a large agenda:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *calendar-mark-days* nil)
#+end_src

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *calendar-week-start-day* 0)
(setf *calendar-months* 12)
(setf *calendar-months-per-row* 3)
#+end_src

** The journal

A journal is a note for each day: what you did, what you thought, what to
remember. ~C-x n j~ opens today's entry, or makes it when there is none. A new
entry is a file with a formatted name (chapter 9), in a folder called
=journal= inside the folder of your notes, with the day as its title, such as
=Friday 20 March 2026=, and the word =journal= among its keywords. It is saved
at once, so there is a file to find later. ~M-x journal-new-entry~ always
makes a new one, even when there is one for today. With ~C-u~ before it, it
asks for a date.

When there is more than one entry for the day, Tuatara asks which you want.
In the calendar, an entry marks its day, and ~n~ opens or makes the entry for
the day at the cursor. You can look back through the month and open any
day.

The journal is a set of ordinary notes, so you can search them, link to
them and export them like any others. Everything about how they are named
and written is a setting.

The folder is =*journal-directory*=. The word that marks an entry, so that
entries can be told from your other notes, is =*journal-keyword*=. An entry
lasts a day as standard. =*journal-interval*= makes it a week, a month or a
year. The kind of file is =*journal-file-type*=, one of =:org=, =:markdown= or
=:text=. =*journal-title-format*= says what the title looks like. It may add
the time, or ask each time. =*journal-file-name*=, when set, gives entries plain
names such as =2026-03-20.org= in place of formatted ones.
=*journal-org-header*= is the text an Org entry starts with.

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *journal-directory* "~/journal/")
(setf *journal-interval* :weekly)
(setf *journal-file-type* :markdown)
#+end_src

The three lines put the entries in =~/journal/=, one for each week, as
Markdown files.

** Odds and ends

The rest of the names that belong to this chapter are small. They are
grouped by what they do.

*** Changing case, and what a character is

Three commands change the case of a word or a selection. They act on the
selection when there is one, and on the next word when there is not.
=capitalize-region= is the same for a selection alone.
=delete-trailing-whitespace= takes the spaces and tabs from the ends of lines.
It works on the selection if there is one and on the whole buffer if not. ~C-x
=~ says which character the cursor is on, with its code in three bases.

#+table: commands upcase-dwim downcase-dwim capitalize-dwim capitalize-region delete-trailing-whitespace what-cursor-position new-empty-buffer

*** Buttons and the mouse

A button is a piece of text that does something when you press ~RET~ on it:
a link in a help page, a name in a list. ~TAB~ goes to the next one in a
buffer and ~S-TAB~ goes back. Moving past the last goes round to the first.
~push-button~ does what the button at the cursor does. It is on ~RET~ and ~l~,
and on ~C-c l~. Links in Markdown are followed with a command of their own.

The mouse works as in other programs. A click puts the cursor, a drag
selects, two clicks select a word and three a line. Letting go of the
button makes what you selected the desktop's primary selection, which the
middle button pastes in Tuatara and in other programs.

#+table: commands forward-button backward-button push-button push-button-at-click markdown-follow-link-at-point mouse-drag-region mouse-release mouse-select-word mouse-select-line

Tuatara picks a mode for a file from its name and what is in it. When it

switches the buffer to Markdown. The commands below are the modes of
written languages, data and system files that no other chapter lists, and a

as soon as you open a file has a name ending in =-mode= like these.

*** Lists and the cursor's popups

When Tuatara lists completions beside the cursor with ~C-M-i~, the faces
below say how the list is drawn. In a buffer you can also have the entries
of a directory listed as you type a path, after the slash.

=*complete-paths*= is =t= by default: type =~/notes/= and the entries of that
folder are listed beside the cursor. Typing narrows the list and ~RET~ puts
the chosen one in. With =nil= nothing is listed. =*complete-paths-modes*=
says it for kinds of buffer, in the form of the pairs of the section "How
lines are shown", and wins over the setting. To list paths in code but not in
Org notes:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *complete-paths-modes* '((org-mode . nil) (prog-mode . t)))
#+end_src

When Tuatara lists files to choose from, it shows when each was changed.
=*relative-age-limit*= is how many seconds back that counts as an age such
as =5 min ago= or =3 days ago=. Older files show the date. The default is
two weeks (1209600). With =0= every file shows its date:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *relative-age-limit* 0)
#+end_src

=*preview-buffers*= is =nil= by default. With =t=, choosing a buffer with
~C-x b~ shows the buffer under the cursor of the list in your window while
you choose, and your own buffer comes back if you give up:

#+begin_src lisp
(setf *preview-buffers* t)
#+end_src

*** Limits and safety

Four settings are limits that keep a mistake from filling your computer. Each
has a sensible standard, and a reason to change it is rare.

- =*insert-file-limit*= is how much of another file ~M-x
  insert-formatted-note-template~ reads. A larger file is refused.
- =*program-output-limit*= is how much of what a program writes Tuatara
  takes. A program that writes more is stopped.
- =*wipe-passes*= is how many times a file that you wipe is overwritten with
  random octets before it is deleted. Once is enough for a drive that writes
  in place.
- =*markdown-toc-on-save*= makes the table of contents of a Markdown file
  again each time you save, under the heading tagged =:TOC:=.

#+table: settings *insert-file-limit* *program-output-limit* *wipe-passes* *markdown-toc-on-save*
